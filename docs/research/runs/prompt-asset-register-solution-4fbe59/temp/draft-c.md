# Sổ Tài Sản Prompt Của Tập Đoàn — The Monday Test

**The answer first.** Build the corporate prompt asset register as a ledger that writes itself, and judge every design decision against one question: does a marketer who is not paid to care about governance open it on Monday instead of the Google Sheet they already have bookmarked? That reframes the entire problem. The competitor is not Langfuse; it is a spreadsheet with a shareable link, instant edit, no permission wall, and zero learning curve. Against that incumbent, feature richness is not an advantage — it is a tax. The winning configuration is narrow and specific: **zero-labor ingestion** so the first session shows the user their *own* prompts already deduplicated rather than a blank state demanding data entry; **exactly one deliberate wall**, at promote-to-production, and nowhere else in the flow; and an approval card that does the reasoning in advance so the approver spends two minutes deciding rather than twenty minutes reconstructing. Everything the evaluation literature establishes about judging subjective work then stops being an academic caveat and becomes a hard design constraint on what the approver is *shown*: a judge score must never be rendered to a non-engineer as a verdict, because on holistic copy tasks the closest studied proxy shows judge rank-order agreement statistically indistinguishable from zero [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass].

The four stated hard constraints survive this reframing, but two of them survive only in a redesigned and honestly-described form. Mandatory human-in-the-loop (HITL) approval stays — grounded in ordinary corporate segregation of duties, not in any present statutory command on the register itself, because neither the EU AI Act's record-keeping article nor Vietnam's Law on AI and its implementing decree obliges a pre-release human sign-off for an internal prompt-management layer [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act][companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]. Role-based access control (RBAC) stays, but pointed at the right artifact: prompt text stays open to marketers, while the eval contract that gates promotion sits behind a stricter role. "Regression eval to guarantee no quality degradation" cannot be honestly implemented as a single automated verdict and must be split three ways — block on deterministic contract properties, report subjective quality as advisory, monitor for provider-side drift on a schedule. And the cost alert must fire on end-to-end cost per successful task, not on prompt-token delta, because a documented case shows a 17% input-token cut *raising* total session cost by 67% [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse].

One honesty requirement runs through everything below. The evidence for this product is weaker than the evidence against it. The positive case is largely vendor-authored, thin, and in the adoption chapter entirely analogical — transferred from customer-relationship-management, data-catalog and campaign-tracker contexts, with no controlled study, no reported sample size, and nothing prompt-register-specific anywhere in the corpus. The negative case is disinterested and strong: a widely-used model registry that reversed its own lifecycle design, configuration-management databases that rot on a named mechanism, data catalogs that fail among business users for a named reason, and feature-store scepticism from practitioners with nothing to sell. That asymmetry is stated plainly here rather than buried, and it shapes the recommendation: build the things the disinterested critics say must be true, and treat the vendor-flavoured promises as marketing until a pilot says otherwise.

## 1. What the Asset Framing Commits You To — And the Prior Art It Inherits

"Sổ tài sản" — asset register, asset ledger — is a *promise to a person*, and only secondarily a claim about accounting. Start with the accounting, dispose of it honestly, and then defend the framing where it actually lives.

A prompt library does not satisfy formal intangible-asset recognition. The four criteria are identifiability, probability of future economic benefits, control over those benefits, and reliable cost measurement; and internally generated know-how-type items are explicitly barred from recognition — training activities, start-up activities, "internally generated goodwill, brands, customer lists and similar items" must be expensed "because such expenditure cannot be distinguished from expenditure to develop the business as a whole" [recognition-and-cost-of-intangible-assets-ias-38-ifrs-community]. A corporate prompt library resembles that excluded category far more than it resembles licensed software. Say this once, plainly, and stop defending it: no prompt will appear on the group's balance sheet because this platform exists.

What the framing *does* commit you to is operational and testable. An asset register carries four things: a named owner, a lifecycle with a retirement path, a record of what the asset is used by, and a cost attached. If those four fields are populated and true at day 90, the name is earned; if they are blank, stale, or filled with a bulk-import default, the product is a database with an expensive name and the objection is fair. This is a demanding standard precisely because it is checkable, and Angle C's reading is that it is *also* the adoption standard, not merely the credibility standard — for a reason the governance sources do not state and which I flag as inference: sourced claim A is that teams revert to the spreadsheet when "no one governs fields, stages, handoffs, or data quality," because "the spreadsheet then feels safer because the team controls the logic directly" [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]; sourced claim B is that a register's operational fields can be derived from actions users are already taking rather than maintained by hand [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github][servicenow-cmdb-best-practices-2026-guide-virima]. Together these imply that the four asset fields are not a governance overhead imposed on the marketer — they are the specific fields whose *absence* causes reversion, and auto-deriving them is how you get trust without asking for labor. No source states that; it follows from both.

The strongest steelman for the framing is practitioner-co-authored rather than vendor-authored, which matters for weighting: "Just as data governance requires a schema, prompt governance requires a definition of what 'good' looks like... Once designed, we lock the prompt together with the specific model version and configuration parameters... This bundle belongs in a shared repository, not a private folder or a DM," and "Prompts are infrastructure now... The sooner prompts are treated as governed digital assets — owned, versioned, tested and measured — the easier it becomes to scale safely" [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio]. The five-pillar skeleton it proposes (purpose/alignment, quality/consistency, lifecycle management, risk/compliance, measurement/enablement) is very close to what this product is building. A parallel trade-press argument reframes the human approval action itself as the asset-generating event: "Every time one of your employees edits an AI-drafted proposal, overrides a recommendation, or approves an exception, they are producing a labeled training example that encodes judgment no public dataset contains... The human-in-the-loop is not oversight. It is the training mechanism" [your-companys-most-valuable-asset-in-the-age-of-ai-is-walking-out-the-door]. That is one columnist's argument, not a study, and should not carry structural weight — but it names the right thing to capture at the promote gate, which is the *reason* for the decision, not just the decision.

The founding premise — that the group's prompts are duplicated and drifting — deserves its correct evidentiary form. The only disinterested empirical measurement in the corpus studies GitHub repositories and reports, at repository level, internal exact duplication in 13.0% (12 of 92) of repositories and internal semantic duplication in 30.4% (28 of 92); external exact duplication in 16.3% (15 of 92) and external semantic in 34.8% (32 of 92); and separately, at prompt level, semantic duplication of 38.5% (9,553 of 24,800) and exact duplication of 10.1% (2,507 of 24,800) [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices]. Two cautions are load-bearing. First, 72.8% of that corpus is public prompt-collection repositories whose whole purpose is aggregating popular prompts, so cross-repository duplication there plausibly reflects benign convergence rather than organizational waste; the internal figures (13.0%/30.4%) are the defensible analogy. Second, that is still a git-structured population — one with version control, code review, and searchable history. The honest argument is *a fortiori*: a population living in Sheets, Notion, Drive and chat threads has none of those affordances, so its duplication is very likely at least as bad. No source measures this for a single organization's document estate; that is an audited absence, and the inference is analogical, not measured.

The prior art the framing inherits is not a set of patterns to copy but a set of failure modes to pre-empt. Configuration-management databases rot on field ownership. Data catalogs fail on entry cost. Model registries got their lifecycle enum wrong and had to reverse it. Feature stores were oversold on justifications that only partly transfer [you-still-dont-need-a-feature-store-xebia]. Inheriting the name means inheriting the graveyard, and a design that does not name each of these and say what stops it is not a design.

## 2. The Asset Record — Prompt Identity, Data Model, and Lifecycle

The schema question that matters is not "which fields" but "who ever types into them." Sort every field into one of three buckets and let the bucket determine the design.

**Tier 1 — auto-derived operational fields. Nobody types these, ever.** `prompt_id` (stable slug, addressable by the SDK); `current_production_version` derived from the promote action itself rather than a manually set stage flag; `owner` derived from who last promoted, falling back to who claimed the record at import; `consumers` derived from software-development-kit fetch calls; `usage_count` and `last_used_at` from telemetry; `cost_30d` computed from traces; `model_binding` (provider, request model, response model). The direct precedent for deriving the production pointer from the action is MLflow's retreat from fixed stages: "Users can transition model versions through four fixed stages (from none, to staging, to production, and then to archived)... Over the years, we've received extensive feedback on the inflexibility of model registry stages," replaced by freeform tags that never gate routing, reassignable aliases where "more than one alias can be applied to any given model version" and reassignment happens "via the UI and client API alike, thereby decoupling model deployment from the production system code," plus separate registered-model objects per environment for access-control separation [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github]. Copy the mechanism *and* the lesson: a widely-deployed registry got its own lifecycle model wrong the first time, so treat any hand-maintained state field as a defect.

The join key for the telemetry half is standardized and does not require capturing prompt content. OpenTelemetry's generative-AI semantic conventions supply `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.output_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.cache_creation.input_tokens` and `gen_ai.usage.cache_read.input_tokens` for usage; `gen_ai.provider.name` (with enum values including `aws.bedrock`, `gcp.vertex_ai`, `openai`, `anthropic`), `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.model` for identity; and `gen_ai.prompt.name` — "The name of the prompt that uniquely identifies it" — as the join key back to the asset record [gen-ai-opentelemetry]. Content-bearing attributes are separately deprecated and privacy-gated: `gen_ai.prompt` is "Removed, no replacement at this time... Deprecated, use Event API to report prompt contents," and `gen_ai.input.messages`/`gen_ai.output.messages` each carry the warning "This attribute is likely to contain sensitive information including user/PII data," with instrumentations permitted to "filter or truncate" them. The cost gate can therefore be built entirely on non-content telemetry — which is not merely a privacy nicety inside a conglomerate but the argument that gets the platform past an internal security review without a data-residency fight.

**Tier 2 — semantic fields nobody can auto-derive.** Purpose ("what is this prompt for, in one sentence a colleague would recognize"), canonicalization judgment (which of these seven near-identical variants is the real one and why), deprecation rationale. These are exactly the fields that decay under a separate-stewardship model. The data-catalog literature names why with unusual precision: automation "primarily captures technical attributes — table names, column types, and system-generated relationships — but it fails to answer critical business questions" such as what the data actually means and how it is used in decision-making; "Without proper contextualization, even the most advanced automation can result in an underutilized, confusing data catalog" [3-data-catalog-pitfalls-to-avoid-for-successful-governance]. That same source supplies the fix in the right shape: rather than expecting manual annotation, "an intelligent catalog should prompt them with structured questions." Fold that structured question into the one moment the user is already stopping — the promote approval — and the field gets captured without creating a stewardship task. That is the two-tier synthesis, and its boundary condition is the report's keystone risk, addressed head-on in section 12.

**Tier 3 — provenance and confidence, which the UI must render, not hide.** Mature configuration-management practice distinguishes "last updated" from "last verified": "'Last updated' timestamps on CI records are often artifacts of a bulk import or administrative edit, not evidence of a verified infrastructure scan," and the fix is to tag "every CI attribute with its discovery source and scan timestamp, giving agents and auditors a clear confidence signal for each value" [servicenow-cmdb-best-practices-2026-guide-virima]. Transfer this literally: every field in the prompt record carries which method populated it (code scan, Sheet connector, chat export, promote action, telemetry) and when. This is not an auditor feature. It is the mechanism that lets the interface show a bulk-imported record *differently from* a governed one — greyed owner, "imported, unclaimed," no green production badge — which is how the register avoids the trap that its own flagship ingestion feature sets for it. (The same source relays an unattributed figure that "over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027, with poor data foundations as the primary cause"; it is vendor-relayed and not independently verified here, and should not be used as evidence.)

The named decay mechanism for registers is field ownership, not tooling: "the single most common finding is exactly this: two systems exist, but neither one is being trusted, because they disagree on the basics — and no one has decided which one wins on which field. The fix is rarely a new tool. It's a one-page 'who owns which column' agreement" [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both]. For this product the two systems are the register and each product's runtime prompt store. The one-page agreement writes itself if Tier 1 is auto-derived: the register owns owner, lifecycle, purpose and cost; the runtime owns nothing that the register displays. Any field where both could write is a future rot site and should be designed out rather than reconciled later.

**Lifecycle states**, chosen so that the ungoverned population is visibly ungoverned: `imported` (discovered, unclaimed, no owner, not servable), `claimed` (a human has said "this is mine"), `draft` (edited, versioned, not live), `production` (a promote decision points here), `deprecated` (superseded, still resolvable for pinned consumers), `retired` (unresolvable; the retirement path an asset register is supposed to have and most registers never build). Note what is deliberately absent: a `staging` stage as a hand-set enum. Environments are labels on versions, not states of the asset — MLflow's own correction [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github].

## 3. Ingestion: Starting From the Existing Chaos

This is the section where the product is won or lost, because it is the only section that happens in the first sixty seconds.

The bar is concrete and stated as a falsifiable claim: if a first-time non-technical user does not see unprompted, editable, recognizably-*theirs* content within roughly the first minute, the win condition — used more than the Google Sheet — is very unlikely to be reached, because a bookmarked Sheet clears that bar trivially. Two independent-domain diagnostics converge on why. Data catalogs fail with business users because "the 'entry ticket' remains too high"; a business manager "isn't looking for a technical table or a database structure. They're looking for a simple answer," yet "current systems still require understanding data vocabulary, navigating complex interfaces, or knowing the technical organisation of data," producing expert dependency in which "teams spend several hours a week simply answering comprehension questions or manually validating metadata" [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. From an unrelated vendor category, the same mechanism is located at population time: "Traditional data catalogs... rely on data teams to do the heavy lifting of manual data entry, holding them responsible for updating the catalog as data assets evolve. This approach is not only time-intensive, but requires significant manual toil that could otherwise be automated" [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery]. Read together, these say the failure is not centralization; it is the unpaid labor of populating the centre, and the technical fluency required to do it. A prompt register whose onboarding is "create your first prompt" has already lost to a Sheet that has 40 prompts in it.

**Three discovery methods, and the register records which one found each record.** The security industry supplies two of them and neither one sees this product's actual population. Static analysis extracts prompt string literals from source before anything runs — abstract-syntax-tree parsing across TypeScript/JavaScript, Python, Go, Rust, Java and C#, producing a "Prompt SBOM: a CycloneDX v1.4 structured inventory of every prompt string in the codebase... The string content and its hash, the source file and line number" — while being explicit about its blind spot: "Dynamic prompt construction. Prompts assembled at runtime from multiple variables, database values, or user inputs are not visible to static analysis by definition," and "Static analysis does not replace runtime screening" [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili]. Egress-boundary discovery observes traffic to known model endpoints but cannot attribute it to an authored, versioned prompt, and sees nothing at all about a prompt sitting unused in a Notion page; its own framing concedes the containment problem — "Blocking one tool only drives employees to another, often through personal accounts or home devices, leaving the enterprise blind to real usage" [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance]. The gap between the two methods is precisely the document-and-chat estate that the problem statement says holds most of the prompts. **Document-source connectors are therefore a structurally required first-class third method, not a convenience** — Google Sheets, Notion, Drive, and chat exports — and the register must stamp each record with its discovery method so coverage claims stay honest rather than implied.

**Clustering: the cheap default is the wrong default here.** MinHash locality-sensitive hashing is the well-known, production-proven approach at scale — one reported deployment "deduplicate[d] 10 billion documents efficiently. Compared to their previous MapReduce-based approach, the new solution more than doubled processing speed and achieved 3-5x cost savings" [minhash-lsh-in-milvus-the-secret-weapon-for-fighting-duplicates-in-llm-training]. But peer-reviewed benchmarking on noisy, adversarial, typo-laden short text — which is exactly the profile of a prompt pasted into chat six times with small edits — shows MinHash collapsing: on a 41-language adversarial/typo benchmark, "RETSim Near-Dup achieves an average Recall@1 of 0.977 across all 41 languages," while "MinHash 0.538" and "SimHash 0.550" (English: MinHash 0.591 versus RETSim Near-Dup 0.987), and on real-world near-duplicate news clustering "RETSim Partial-Dup outperforms all other approaches by a significant margin (4.8% ARI compared to our best MinHash result)" [retsim-resilient-and-efficient-text-similarity]. The cost is real — the neural method is reported "46x slower" than MinHash on CPU and competitive only on GPU — but the trade is correct for this population, and there is a cheaper middle path with independent precedent: embedding-based semantic similarity at a high cosine threshold, which is what the GitHub duplication study itself used (>0.95 on all-mpnet-base-v2) [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices]. Design rule: MinHash as a cheap pre-filter for exact and near-exact matches, embeddings for the semantic cluster the demo actually depends on. A dedup pipeline built only on MinHash will under-cluster the messy copy-pasted-with-edits population the sweep exists to find, and the demo's central visual — "here are your seven versions of the same prompt" — will render as seven unrelated rows.

**The ingestion agent's tools must be built for a context budget, not for completeness.** The applicable discipline is stated by a primary engineering source: "A common error we've observed is tools that merely wrap existing software functionality or API endpoints... if an LLM agent uses a tool that returns ALL contacts and then has to read through each one token-by-token, it's wasting its limited context space," with the prescription to implement "some combination of pagination, range selection, filtering, and/or truncation with sensible default parameter values" and a concrete precedent of restricting tool responses to 25,000 tokens by default [writing-effective-tools-for-ai-agentsusing-ai-agents-anthropic]. For ingestion this means the agent never reads a whole Notion workspace into context; it reads a paginated candidate list produced by deterministic extraction and clustering, and is used only where judgment is required — naming a cluster, drafting a purpose sentence, flagging a likely secret or personal-data leak in an imported prompt.

**The counterweight, stated so it is not smuggled past.** The same catalog literature that condemns manual entry also condemns pure automation: relying only on automation in curation "results in metadata that lacks business context, making it difficult for users to derive value" [3-data-catalog-pitfalls-to-avoid-for-successful-governance]. Zero-labor ingestion is therefore correct as an *onboarding* strategy and insufficient as a *governance* strategy — which is the collision this report must resolve rather than dodge, and does, in sections 6 and 12: import everything with no questions asked, but import into a state that is visibly not part of the governed ledger, and make the first meaningful interaction with a record — claiming it, editing it, or promoting it — the moment the one structured question gets asked.

**And ingestion is not a one-off.** The moment a new prompt is written today is inside a chat thread, a Sheet cell, or a Notion block — not inside a purpose-built tool. Unless there is a lightweight embedded capture point at the point of creation (a browser "save to register" action, or a forward-to-bot pattern in whatever chat channel the group actually uses), new prompts keep being born outside, and the register re-accumulates the exact problem it launched to solve. The creative-operations diagnostic names this as a category that scales faster than its predecessor: "AI tools used individually by team members without integration into the shared production environment are a new category of shadow workflow... When every team member uses their own AI, the shadow data problem scales significantly faster than it did in the spreadsheet era" [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog]. Whether an embedded capture surface measurably reduces new shadow-prompt creation, versus simply adding one more place to fragment across, is asserted here by analogy and is untested in any source read — flag it as the product's largest unvalidated bet after ingestion itself.

## 4. Versioning, Release, and Rollback Mechanics

The architectural fork — git-style branching versus a movable label pointer — is settled by the persona choice, but the losing side names a cost that must be paid explicitly.

Every observed implementation converges on the same primitive. Langfuse: "To 'deploy' a prompt version, you have to assign the label `production`... to that prompt version," and "You can quickly rollback to a previous version by setting the `production` label to that previous version" [version-control-langfuse]. PromptLayer is mechanically identical — "hover over a prompt template version, click 'Add Release Label'" [release-labels-promptlayer]. Bedrock creates a `DRAFT` and snapshots flat numbered versions retrieved by version number [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock][view-information-about-versions-of-a-prompt-in-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock]. Vertex exposes `create_version`, `list_versions` and `restore_version` inside a single Google Cloud project [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. The reason to adopt this shape is not fashion: a movable label is what lets a non-developer publish and roll back without a deploy, which is the operative requirement here, and git-based approaches are described as unsuitable where content changes rapidly [api-first-cms-vs-git-based].

The uncompensated cost: label-based versioning gives a linear history with movable environment pointers, not divergent branches with reconciliation. The opposing case is real — databases are described as not supporting sophisticated versioning, distributed parallel multi-environment workflows, or branching, all ordinary enterprise content requirements, with straightforward revert as a git advantage [git-based-cms-vs-api-driven-cms-which-headless-cms-should-you-choose-bejamas]. Two people editing the same prompt in different directions produce either colliding linear versions or, far more likely inside a marketing team, a second near-duplicate asset under a slightly different name — and *nothing in the versioning primitive catches that*. Only the register's own duplicate clustering catches it, after the fact. This converts an admitted weakness into the strongest argument for the ingestion capability being load-bearing rather than an onboarding convenience: **near-duplicate clustering is the standing compensating control for an architecture that cannot merge**, and it must therefore run continuously against the live register, not only at import.

Angle C's specific contribution here is about *undo*, and it is an inference stated as such. Sourced claim A: the mandatory promotion wall is a stated hard constraint and the one piece of friction the register deliberately keeps [interim adoption position; consistent with the problem statement]. Sourced claim B: rollback in a label architecture is a single pointer reassignment that the same non-developer can perform, with the application picking up the change on its next fetch [version-control-langfuse][caching-langfuse]. Together they imply the design rule that makes the wall tolerable: **the promote gate is only defensible to a non-engineer if undo is one click and takes effect in seconds.** A wall you cannot back out of gets routed around; a wall with a visible, instant escape hatch gets used. No source states this; it follows from both, and it has a concrete UI consequence — the rollback control belongs on the prompt's main view, not buried in a version-history submenu, and it must not itself require a second approval.

Three further mechanics follow from the persona.

*No save button.* The prompt body is a single large freeform field with silent autosave; every meaningful pause creates a new immutable version automatically. This is how you approximate the spreadsheet's instant-edit affordance without abandoning immutable versioning: the structure exists underneath, but the user never performs a "commit" gesture. This is also the demo's second beat.

*Two required fields at creation, maximum.* Name/slug (needed for API/SDK addressability) and body. Everything else — owner, tags, source, purpose — is auto-suggested from ingestion context or drafted by the agent, and is editable but never blocking. A form with eight required fields is a Sheet-reversion generator.

*The register's own served contract needs a declared compatibility mode.* Downstream product teams will build against whatever the SDK returns, and the register will want to add fields. The schema-registry taxonomy is directly reusable: "Backward — consumers using new schema can read data written with old schema (add optional fields, remove fields). Forward — consumers using old schema can read data written with new schema... Full — both backward and forward compatible... The default compatibility mode is BACKWARD" [schema-evolution-compatibility-types-backward-forward-full-transitive-confluent]. Declare BACKWARD for the served prompt payload and publish it. This costs nothing now and prevents the failure where a metadata addition breaks three products' deserializers on a Tuesday, which is the kind of event that gets an internal platform banned.

## 5. Regression Eval You Can Actually Gate On

The constraint as written — "eval hồi quy để đảm bảo không giảm chất lượng," regression eval to guarantee no quality degradation — cannot be honestly implemented as a single automated verdict for this product's primary workload. Saying so is not a retreat; the three-part rule that replaces it is a better answer than the constraint as written, and it is the section where the design earns credibility with the engineers who will have to operate it.

**Why the single-verdict version fails, in the specific case of a marketer's output.** The decisive evidence is a controlled multi-format study that separates absolute error from *discriminative validity* — the ability to correctly rank-order responses by quality — and shows the two can come apart entirely. On structured questions (n=771), models "achieve fractional mean absolute errors (fMAE) ≈0.22 with robust discriminative validity (Spearman ρ>0.6)," rising to committee ρ=0.885 when official solutions are supplied; on scientific plots (n=1400), ρ>0.84. On essays marked holistically 0–100 (n=55 scripts / 275 essays), "blind AI marking is harsher and more variable than human marking, with discriminative validity already poor (ρ≈0.1). Adding a mark scheme does not improve discrimination (ρ≈0; all confidence intervals include zero)" — committee values of 0.088 blind, −0.044 with scheme, 0.034 with scheme plus anchor, and "every single one of these 95% CIs includes zero." The reason is the task, not the model: "The average human–human MAE was 6.84 marks... rank-order agreement between human markers was very limited, with an average pairwise Spearman correlation of just ρ=0.054... even expert human markers fail to reach consensus on rank ordering." The paper's deployment diagnostic is the sentence to build the product around: "Before asking whether LLM-as-a-judge can be used to mark a task, one should first establish whether the task is sufficiently criterion-referenceable that human markers themselves assign stable and discriminative judgements" [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass]. Marketing copy, brand voice and design briefs sit at the essay end of that axis by construction — no official solution, no single correct derivation, and creative directors routinely disagreeing about what counts as good. The analogy from physics essays to marketing copy is a proxy, not a measurement; no source in this corpus studies judge validity on marketing copy at all, and that absence is a finding, not a gap to paper over.

**The strongest opposing evidence, at full force.** The widely cited high-water mark reports that "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans" [230605685-judging-llm-as-a-judge-with-mt-bench-and-chatbot-arena]. That figure is real and describes curated general-chat preference data under its original protocol. It is not reproduced as a floor by more recent, adversarially-aware work: after testing nine debiasing strategies across five judges, the best configuration of any tested reaches "71.0%, κ=0.549, p<0.0001" at about $0.001 per evaluation, and the most common debiasing technique actively backfires where it matters most — "On LLMBar's adversarial instances, position swap consistently and significantly hurts all models (−3 to −13 pp)." Residual style bias dominates: "Four of five models show strong style bias (Pro +0.76, Flash +0.72, Claude +0.68, Llama +0.40)," and a human check "found humans prefer markdown only 57% of the time versus 73–97% for four of five judges" [judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm]. A ceiling of 71% on general chat preference, and near-zero rank information on holistic copy, is not a foundation for the word "guarantee."

**The design consequence, which is Angle C's core contribution: never show a marketer or an approver a judge score as a verdict.** If a number carries no rank information, rendering it as "Quality: 82 (+6) ✅" manufactures false confidence in exactly the person least equipped to discount it. This is an inference with stated provenance: sourced claim A is the near-zero discriminative validity on the essay-analogue task with confidence intervals spanning zero [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass]; sourced claim B is the practitioner finding that teams "gather evaluation data but cannot translate findings into concrete improvements," and that interpretive, hands-on practices "are necessary adaptations to LLM characteristics rather than methodological failures" [260416304-results-actionability-gap-understanding-how-practitioners-evaluate-llm]. Together they imply that the correct rendering of the subjective signal is not a score but *evidence the human can adjudicate*: side-by-side outputs on the handful of production inputs where old and new versions diverge most, with the judge used only to *select which examples to surface*, never to declare a winner. Selecting divergent cases is a retrieval task, not a ranking task, and is therefore not subject to the validity collapse. Vendor practice independently confirms that side-by-side comparison is the interaction non-engineers actually use: "A comparison mode puts two prompt variants in separate columns. You enter the same input, run both, and see how the outputs differ. This is how product managers and domain experts can do real prompt engineering without writing code" [prompt-management-for-non-engineers-how-product-teams-can-own-their-ai-prompts-a].

**What you can block on: contract, not quality.** The cleanest statement of this in the corpus comes from a practitioner who deliberately abandoned the judge route: "regression testing isn't really a quality problem — it's a contract problem. Checking if the output intent matches the expected intent is binary, so a judge model just adds noise" [prompt-engineering-fails-quietly-prompt-regression-is-why-towards-data-science]. That source also supplies the reason a single scalar eval-delta is insufficient even for deterministic checks: a version "improved overall accuracy by 10% while negation-handling collapsed 66.7%, from 100.0%→33.3%." The implementable structure is risk-category contracts with per-category thresholds — an approach that "organizes evaluation prompts into risk-aligned groups that represent distinct deployment concerns. Each risk category is evaluated independently to detect domain-specific drift that aggregate metrics can mask," with worked thresholds such as a pass rate above 95% for one category, 100% format compliance for structured output, and 0% security violations [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. For a marketing prompt the analogous categories are concrete and checkable: banned-claims list, required legal disclaimer present, brand-name spelling, output length bounds, structured-field schema conformance, and no personal data echoed. These are the properties on which the gate can *block*, and they are also the properties a marketer intuitively accepts as blocking — nobody argues with "this version dropped the mandatory disclaimer."

**What you must monitor, because no pre-release gate can see it.** Provider-side drift is categorically outside the release cycle. The documented magnitudes belong to Chen, Zaharia & Zou (2024, *Harvard Data Science Review*), "How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time?": GPT-4's accuracy on identifying prime numbers dropped from 84% to 51% between the March and June 2023 versions, and direct execution of generated code fell from 52% to 10% within three months, without any version change — relayed and correctly attributed in the corpus [prompt-drift-what-it-is-and-how-to-detect-it-agenta-blog][test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. A corroborating incident: "Anthropic's August 2025 postmortem report documents three bugs affecting Claude Sonnet 4's performance without any API modifications, including routing errors affecting up to 16% of requests and output corruption causing non-ASCII character injection" [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. A first-person practitioner account independently reproduces the shape in a different toolchain — evals passing in continuous integration, a silent provider model change 21 days later breaking JSON extraction on roughly 15% of calls with zero CI trigger, and the general principle "PromptFoo catches regressions at step 1. It doesn't catch steps 2-6... Neither does the other's job" [promptfoo-passes-production-still-breaks-heres-the-gap-dev-community]. The register's answer is to re-run the *same* deterministic contract against the currently-serving model on a schedule, turning the compatibility check into a recurring job rather than a promotion-time checkpoint. Note the honest limitation the same framework flags: threshold calibration lacks principled statistical grounding, per-prompt run-to-run variability is real ("Some responses passed all checks in one run and failed in another"), and providers rarely expose version identifiers, so drift attribution operates under partial observability [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain].

**Bootstrapping the eval set, and who owns it.** The organization has no test set, so the contract must be built from production traffic — sample real inputs, keep the ones where the prompt is actually used, and let the human confirm a handful of expected properties rather than write expected outputs. Judge alignment, where a judge is used at all, is a maintained system and not a setup step: one reported process logged "the LLM response, judge's critique (i.e., CoT), and final outcome," reviewed them with stakeholders, and "Over three iterations, agreement with humans and LLM improved from 68% to 94%," while also naming criteria drift as a first-class failure mode — "developers' perceptions of what constitutes 'good' and 'bad' outputs shift as they interact with more data... these predefined criteria are often incomplete" [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms]. The corollary is blunt: "Model-based evaluation is a meta-problem within your larger problem. You must maintain a mini-evaluation system to track its quality" [your-ai-product-needs-evals-hamels-blog]. This is the argument for pointing RBAC at the contract rather than only at the text, developed in the next section. One architectural warning belongs here: self-preference bias tracks output familiarity rather than an identifiable self-signal — judges "assign significantly higher evaluations to outputs with lower perplexity than human evaluators, regardless of whether the outputs were self-generated" [241021819-self-preference-bias-in-llm-as-a-judge] — so an agent that both proposes prompt edits and judges the resulting outputs is structurally exposed unless the judge and the improver are deliberately drawn from different model families. That is a one-line configuration decision with a real integrity payoff.

## 6. The Promote Gate — HITL, RBAC, and the Two-Minute Decision

The gate is the product's one deliberate wall. It must survive three attacks: the delivery-performance attack, the rubber-stamp attack, and the "you have loaded four jobs onto one beam" attack. It survives all three, but only in a specific shape, and only if one of the four jobs is explicitly demoted.

**Attack one: approval makes delivery worse.** Give it full force. "no evidence was found to support the hypothesis that a more formal, external review process was associated with lower change fail rates" [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval]; "organizations with formal external approval processes were found to be 2.6 times more likely to be low performers," and, quoting *Accelerate* directly, "We found that external approvals were negatively correlated with lead time, deployment frequency, and restore time, and had no correlation with change fail rate... It is, in fact, worse than having no change approval process at all"; with UK regulator data across more than a million production changes showing that "CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, and in some firms the CAB had not rejected a single change during 2019" [when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz]. That is a serious finding and it is not being waved away. What it measured, precisely, is approval by a body *external* to the change, lacking context, expected to reconstruct the reasoning itself. The same research programme's prescribed remedy is not removing the gate: "Use peer review to meet the goal of segregation of duties, with reviews, comments, and approvals captured in the team's development platform as part of the development process" [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval]. An accountable owner approving from a pre-computed evidence card inside the platform *is* that remedy, structurally. The gate survives by being embedded and evidenced, not by being defended as tradition.

**Attack two: it becomes theatre.** The throughput mechanism is real and separately argued: reviewers at large organisations already "spend between ten and fifteen percent of their working hours reading and commenting on others' code," and as volume rises "reviews of agent-generated code become rubber-stamps: the human approves because the code looks correct, because the tests pass, and because the cognitive cost of genuine scrutiny is prohibitive" — with the carve-out that "Human approval is not eliminated; it is reserved for the decisions that genuinely require it: high-risk changes, novel architecture choices, and regulated code paths where a named human must bear legal accountability" [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]. The qualitative failure signatures are catalogued — "PRs approved within minutes of being opened," "Repetitive 'LGTM' or 'Looks good' comments with no substance," "Reviewers frequently approving code they have not read" — and, importantly, **no quantified size threshold exists in that source; any "400-line" or "1000-line" figure attributed to it would be fabricated** [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]. Two defences follow. Rate discipline: at most one active candidate version per prompt awaiting a decision, never a batch of variants, and no candidate enters the queue until it has already cleared the deterministic contract. And instrumentation: track time-to-decision and override rate as leading indicators, on the same logic that "Very short latency combined with fast approvals may indicate reviews are not happening at all" [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]. A register that cannot tell you its own median approval time is not measuring the only control it has.

**Attack three: the beam is overloaded.** Four separate jobs have converged on this one moment — read a quality signal, exercise judgment automation cannot supply, confirm AI-drafted semantic metadata, and check cost and blast radius — and doing all four in two minutes is not obviously possible. Resolve this by *ordering and defaulting*, not by asserting the card is well-designed.

- **Job 1, blocking, zero human effort: the contract.** Deterministic category checks run before the card is ever generated. A failing candidate never reaches the approver. The card shows a single line: "Contract: 6/6 categories pass" or a named failure. This is the only job that blocks.
- **Job 2, pre-computed, glanceable: blast radius and cost.** Which products fetch this prompt, how many calls in the last 30 days, and the modelled cost delta per successful task. Both are derived from telemetry the register already holds; the approver reads, does not compute.
- **Job 3, pre-computed, adjudicable: divergence examples.** Three to five real production inputs where old and new outputs differ most, side by side, with a consequence-language diff above them ("this version stops asking for the customer's budget"). No score. This is the job the human is uniquely qualified for and the eval literature says automation cannot do.
- **Job 4, demoted and defaulted: semantic capture.** One field — purpose — pre-drafted by the agent, editable in place, **asked only on the first promotion of a new prompt, not on every version.** On subsequent promotions the field is displayed read-only with a small "not right?" edit affordance. This is the concession Tension 1 demands: if all four jobs compete for the same two minutes, semantic capture is the one that degrades under load, so it must be the one that is defaulted rather than required.

**What the card looks like when the approver does nothing but click approve:** the contract passed, the production pointer moves, the owner is set to the approver, the purpose field keeps the agent's draft with provenance marked `agent-drafted, unconfirmed`, and the decision record stores the card exactly as rendered. Nothing silently becomes true that was not true. That last property is the one that distinguishes a real approval object from a label write.

**The approval-decision object is the market gap, confirmed rather than assumed.** Across four independently-read vendor surfaces, promotion is everywhere a role-gated write to a pointer, with no pending-review state, no evidence record, no reviewer distinct from actor, and no rejection path. Langfuse's protected labels are access control over a label, not a workflow: "Protected prompt labels give project admins and owners... the ability to prevent labels from being modified or deleted," but "admin and owner roles can still modify or delete the label, effectively changing the production prompt version" [version-control-langfuse]. PromptLayer's release labels are a direct UI write [release-labels-promptlayer]. Bedrock creates a `DRAFT` and gates by IAM permission [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock]. Vertex offers create/list/restore version methods inside one project [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. The confirmation runs to database-schema granularity: as late as Nov 2024–Jan 2025 there was no API to update a prompt label, and a user's "temporary solution" was raw SQL against a Postgres `prompts` table (`UPDATE prompts SET labels = ...`), with a maintainer confirming label updates finally shipped "via our API / SDKs" in Jan 2025 [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]. For over a year there was a labels *column*, never an approval *object*. This is the single strongest differentiation claim available, and it is negative evidence of an unusually solid kind — four independent sources, zero counter-examples.

**RBAC, pointed at the right artifact.** The conventional design gates who may edit prompt text. That is the wrong boundary. Prompt-text editing is low-stakes and fully reversible via versioning, and gating it is precisely the friction that drives a marketer back to a Sheet. The consequential artifact is the eval contract — the thresholds and the critical-category list — because criteria themselves drift and are as unstable and as consequential as the prompts they gate [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms]. So: **anyone in the group may create, edit, version, and run evals on prompts. Promotion to production requires the accountable owner role. Changing a threshold or adding/removing a critical category requires a stricter, reviewed platform-governance role.** That is a two-key structure that a non-engineer will tolerate because the key they are denied is one they never wanted. Note honestly that no source in the corpus studies prompt-registry RBAC design directly; this is inference from the criteria-drift and eval-bottleneck literature, not an observed governance case study.

**An emergency path is mandatory, not optional.** A break-glass promote — available to the owner, logged with a required one-line reason, and generating a review task due within 24 hours — is not a weakening of the gate. It is what prevents the gate from being routed around entirely, on the same logic that a system which "adds more steps than it removes" produces a shadow process rather than compliance [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. A gate with no sanctioned exception acquires an unsanctioned one.

**The legal basis, stated honestly.** Vietnam's regime is real and has two distinct clocks that must not be conflated. The Law on Artificial Intelligence takes effect 1 March 2026, "with grace periods for most existing AI system providers and deployers lasting until 1 March 2027 (or 1 September 2027 for AI systems in the fields of health, education and finance)" [vietnam-artificial-intelligence-law-foundation-and-outlook-insight-baker-mckenzi], independently corroborated as an 18-month grace period for finance, healthcare and education and 12 months for all other sectors [vietnam-the-first-law-on-artificial-intelligence-what-you-must-know-duane-morris]. Separately, the implementing decree imposes a notification deadline: "For any AI system put into operation before March 1, 2026... Within 60 days from the effective date of the Decree (i.e., before June 30, 2026), transitioning organizations and enterprises must submit a notice on the one-stop portal along with a transition plan or compliance scheme" [update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi]. Both are real; they attach to different obligations. But neither regime commands a pre-release human sign-off on an internal prompt-management layer. Vietnam's high-risk deployer obligations require operators to "ensure safety, data security and maintain the possibility of human intervention during use" — a runtime override capacity, not an upstream approval gate — alongside documentation, logging and accountability duties [companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]. The EU's parallel provision is a logging mandate on the deployed system: "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system" [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act]. **The honest basis for mandatory HITL is corporate segregation of duties plus prophylactic readiness for the group's downstream products becoming regulated AI systems.** That is more durable than a legal claim that would not survive counsel review — and it is also a better sales argument internally, because the approval record is exactly the artifact those downstream products will need when their own grace period expires in March or September 2027.

## 7. Cost and Token Economics as a Release Control

Cost is the one number a marketer, a manager and a finance business partner all understand without training, which makes it the register's most effective vehicle for governance that does not feel like governance. It is also the constraint most easily implemented wrong.

**The naive implementation is worse than no gate.** "Alert when a change increases token usage/cost" reads as "alert on prompt-token delta." That gate can be confidently wrong in both directions. The counter-example is specific and well-instrumented: "aggressive compression reduced input tokens by 17 percent and still increased total session cost by 67 percent. The model had less text to read and more work to do... Now it pays for that recovery with extra reasoning, extra retries, and extra correction loops" — the author's name for it is "the compression tax" [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse]. Give the other side its strongest form: compression research reports "up to 20x compression with under 2% quality loss on benchmarks including CoQA, HotpotQA, and TriviaQA," and a selective-context method achieving "50% context reduction while maintaining comparable performance: only 0.023 degradation in BERTScore" [prompt-compression-cut-token-costs-without-losing-quality-neuraltrust] — though that source is vendor marketing relaying research it did not conduct, and should be read as relaying rather than establishing those figures. A formal treatment establishes that a real compression/fidelity frontier exists and that "there is a large gap between the performance of current prompt compression methods and the optimal strategy," while emphasising "the criticality of query-aware prompt compression" [240715504-fundamental-limits-of-prompt-compression-a-rate-distortion-framework-f]. Both sides are right about different measures. The claim to make is narrow and fully supported: **input-token delta is not a valid proxy for cost delta.** That is all the design decision requires.

**The measure that is correct: cost per successfully completed task, over a traffic sample, attributed to the prompt version, including retries and multi-turn recovery.** The implementable architecture exists in adjacent gateway practice: per-request metadata tagging where "`X-TFY-METADATA` is a JSON object the application sets per-request. The gateway stores it on the trace and projects a curated subset to low-cardinality metric labels," with "Per-trace cost... computed at span close from `gen_ai.usage` attributes and a versioned pricing table," and two-tier enforcement — "Soft alerts at 80% via PagerDuty or Slack, and hard limits that return HTTP 429 with a `budget_exhausted` error." A worked graceful-degradation example: "At 80% the team is bumped from Sonnet to Haiku — a 3x cost reduction... The team owner is notified at each transition. The pipeline does not stop" [llm-cost-attribution-at-scale-metadata-tagging-team-budgets-and-chargeback-repor]. That source also flags an arithmetic trap worth reproducing verbatim in the implementation notes: "gen_ai.usage.input_tokens is defined as the total including both cache lines, so subtracting once for cache_read but not for cache_write... double-counts the cache-write portion." Joined against `gen_ai.prompt.name` [gen-ai-opentelemetry], this gives per-prompt, per-version, per-provider cost attribution without touching prompt content.

**How it appears to the two personas who matter.** To the editor, ambient and non-blocking: a small figure beside the editor showing modelled monthly cost at current traffic, updating as they type, denominated in VND per month rather than tokens per call. To the approver, binding: a cost line on the evidence card with a configured threshold above which promotion requires an explicit acknowledgement, not merely a display. The distinction between "shown while editing" and "binding at promotion" is the whole of the third USP pillar and it is cheap to build once telemetry exists.

Angle C's interpretive claim here: the cost gate is the register's Trojan horse. Everything else the platform does — versioning, approval, audit — is governance the marketing team did not ask for. Cost is the one thing that makes the register *useful to them* in an argument with their own budget holder, which converts the ledger from a compliance artifact into a bargaining chip. That is the mechanism by which a governance platform becomes something people open voluntarily, and it is the strongest reason to build cost attribution before RBAC granularity rather than after.

Two honest limits. First, cost per successful task requires a definition of "successful," which for a marketing prompt is not automatically available — the practical proxy is completion without a contract violation and without a human re-run within the session, which is a defensible heuristic and not a measurement. Second, the evidence base for the compression counter-example is a single well-instrumented practitioner report against two sources including a formal analysis; the narrow claim is well supported, a broader "compression does not work" claim is not.

## 8. Distribution to Products — API/SDK, Parity, and Failure Modes

The consuming developer is persona #3 and has the least influence on whether the marketer adopts the register — right up until the moment the register causes a production incident, at which point that developer's team bans it and the marketer's tool dies with it. Distribution is therefore an adoption problem wearing an infrastructure costume.

**The reliability bar is specific and non-negotiable.** The documented pattern for a prompt-serving layer is: client-side caching so "there's no latency impact after the first use and no availability risk," stale-while-revalidate semantics where "stale prompts are served immediately while it revalidates in the background," and a layered server cache with database fallback — "The API caches prompts in a Redis cache... if Redis is unavailable, the database serves as backup" [caching-langfuse]. Even with all of that, one failure mode remains: the fetch "will throw an exception if: No local (fresh or stale) cached prompt is available... **and** network request fails... after retries," mitigated by pre-fetching at startup and exiting on failure, or by "a hardcoded fallback prompt compiled into the client and used when both the local cache is empty and the [platform] API is unavailable" [guaranteed-availability-langfuse]. This shape is not one vendor's idiosyncrasy: an entirely unrelated domain arrives at it by convergent design, prescribing that products "continue to function smoothly even if a Remote Config fetch is delayed, times out, or returns an error," that teams "Provide a local default for every Remote Config key," that the last successful fetch is cached locally and served when a subsequent fetch fails, and explicitly warning against fetching every frame or "Retrying immediately on failure" [what-is-the-recommended-way-to-handle-remote-config-fetch-failures-unity-support]. Two independent categories converging on cache-plus-TTL-plus-async-refresh-plus-hardcoded-fallback is about as strong as mechanism evidence gets.

**The architectural consequence: the register must not sit in the hot path.** Push promoted versions down into each product's existing runtime store — Bedrock's prompt management for products already there, Vertex's for products already there, a lightweight store for self-hosted runtimes — and pull usage back via OpenTelemetry. The register becomes the control plane and the record of who approved what and why; the runtime remains the serving path. This is also what makes the aggregation claim true rather than aspirational, because the runtimes cannot aggregate themselves: Bedrock is addressed within a single account and console session [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock], and Vertex is explicit that its capabilities operate "within your Google Cloud project," with `list()` retrieving prompts "in the current project" [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. Buying a larger plan from either vendor does not create a cross-provider view. Flag the boundary honestly: neither doc set was checked against cross-account resource sharing within a single cloud, so the claim is firm *across* providers and not fully verified *within* one provider's multi-account estate.

**Parity is the credibility contract, and it is an adoption issue.** If the register displays version 7 as production and the SDK serves version 5 — because someone edited the runtime store directly through a cloud console — then the register's central claim, "you can see which version is running," is false, and the first person to discover it will go back to keeping a Sheet. The two-source-of-truth failure is the exact rot mechanism the configuration-management literature names, where two systems disagree and nobody decided which wins on which field [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both]. Three controls follow: the register reconciles against each runtime on a schedule and surfaces divergence as a first-class alert on the prompt's own page; direct console edits to pushed prompts are discouraged by convention and *detected* by reconciliation rather than assumed away; and the register's served payload declares BACKWARD compatibility so consumers on different SDK versions have a documented guarantee rather than an implicit one [schema-evolution-compatibility-types-backward-forward-full-transitive-confluent].

**Version pinning and test/prod parity.** Consuming products fetch by label (`production`) by default and may pin an explicit version for regulated or high-blast-radius paths; a pinned consumer continues to resolve a `deprecated` version and stops resolving a `retired` one, which is what makes the retirement path in section 2 real rather than cosmetic. Test environments fetch the same asset by a different label, so a staging product never accidentally serves an unapproved version — the movable-label mechanism handles this natively [version-control-langfuse][release-labels-promptlayer].

## 9. The Agent Layer and the Build / Buy / Layer Decision

The problem statement mandates an agent that proposes improvements. The positioning explicitly declines to lead with that capability, calling it commoditized. Both are right, and the resolution is to narrow what "propose" means rather than to drop it — while redirecting the agent's main effort at the thing that actually determines adoption.

**What the agent should mostly do: translate, not optimize.** For a non-engineer, the highest-leverage agent function is collapsing the entry ticket. The data-catalog diagnosis is that catalogs became "a tool reserved for data teams" because business users must understand "data vocabulary, navigating complex interfaces, or knowing the technical organisation of data," and its own prescription is to use conversational/AI interfaces to lower that barrier and to "Think usage before documentation" [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. Applied here, the agent's core jobs are: naming a near-duplicate cluster in human language; drafting the one-sentence purpose field so the human confirms rather than composes; and rendering a version diff in *consequence language* ("this version no longer asks for the customer's budget and adds a discount claim") rather than as a character-level diff a marketer cannot parse. None of that is optimization. All of it directly attacks the mechanism that kills adjacent registries.

**What the agent should do sparingly: propose edits, gated hard.** The quantitative case against a free-running improver is strong. Generic, well-intentioned prompt additions "do not produce monotonic improvements: stronger output-contract prompts improve strict extraction for both models, while RAG citation/content-compliance declines under some generic-rule conditions. The largest observed decline occurs for Qwen 2.5 on RAG when generic rules are appended to the user prompt, from 26/30 to 9/30" — an 86.7% to 30% pass rate — with a named mechanism: "The improved prompt's instruction to 'be helpful' and 'provide comprehensive answers' encouraged the models to hallucinate information not present in the source documents" [260122025-when-generic-prompt-improvements-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for][when-better-prompts-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for-llm-applications-a-fram]. Crucially the harm is dimension-specific: the same generic edit that wrecked grounding *improved* instruction-following. There is no scalar "better prompt." The paper itself calls these "existence proofs (generic prompts *can* hurt) rather than universal quantitative claims" on small synthetic suites with two local quantized models — so take the mechanism, not the percentages. Layered on top, the dominant self-reflection paradigm behind most auto-optimizers is critiqued at the level of its own causal story: "While such methods often result in a performance improvement, our experiments suggest that the gradient analogy does not accurately explain their behavior" [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat]. **Audited absence: only the abstract of that paper was ever retrieved; both full-text fetches failed.** Its argument is used only at the level an abstract supports — namely, that the rationale an agent attaches to an edit is a post-hoc narrative rather than a derivation, which is sufficient to justify never showing the approver a self-narrated rationale as evidence.

The shipped competitor capability is itself gated, which undercuts the idea that raw auto-proposal is a differentiator worth competing on: optimization fires from human annotations — "After adding thumbs up/down reactions or free text annotations to playground outputs, select Optimize to open Loop with your annotations as context" — and consequential actions are confirmation-gated [loop-agent-braintrust]. An independent open-source implementer applies classic machine-learning discipline instead: "the labeled dataset is split into train, validation, and test subsets (by default, 40% train, 40% validation, and 20% test — all configurable)... This ensures that even if a prompt overfits the training examples, it will fail on validation and be discarded," with the optimizer's own template instructed to "Generalize examples to not overfit on them" [how-we-built-open-source-automated-prompt-optimization]. **Audited absence: that source reports no quantified train/test generalization gap** — the split is asserted as design intent, not measured. Both implementers treat the gate as load-bearing, which is itself evidence that the bare capability is considered unsafe by the people shipping it.

So: the improvement proposal fires only for a prompt that already has a task-specific contract, only when a failure cluster or a cost/latency signal crosses a declared threshold, and only after the candidate has cleared a held-out slice of that same suite. At most one active candidate per prompt at a time. What reaches the human is the evidence card, never a raw candidate with a self-narrated justification.

**Build / buy / layer.** The irreducible built surface is four components: the cross-provider asset schema, the approval-decision object (not a label write), the per-runtime push adapters, and the OpenTelemetry telemetry ingestion mapped to non-content attributes. Everything else — storage, version numbering, draft states, playground UI, distributed caching — is commodity that vendors already operate at a standard a small team cannot match on a short timeline. The build-vs-buy framing supports exactly this split: where requirements are not bespoke, reuse; where the capability is structurally unavailable, build; and the piece is blunt about the trap in between — "It's straightforward enough to get a version of whatever you're building working on one person's computer. What next? Where is the app going to live?... Who's going to take responsibility for updating libraries, or maintaining test cases?" [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. The maintenance economics point the same way: every built surface becomes a standing liability someone must staff, and the discipline "by reducing a specific tech debt, can I increase developer productivity and deliver business value right now? If I can't, then I don't pay it down" applies in reverse to new build decisions [paying-down-tech-debt-by-gergely-orosz-and-lou-franco].

**The tension this creates, and its resolution.** The three things that make this product distinctive — the ingestion/clustering agent, the embedded capture surfaces, and the deduplication that compensates for the missing merge semantics — all sit *outside* the four sanctioned surfaces. A layer thin enough to be maintainable may be too thin to be worth adopting. Resolve by sequencing rather than arbitration: the differentiators get built first and thinly (a connector for one document source, not five; a clustering pass, not a clustering platform), while the four sanctioned surfaces are the ones built to last. And note the convergence that makes the sequencing easy: **the approval-decision object is simultaneously the sanctioned bespoke surface, the confirmed market gap across four vendors, and the agent's output target.** One component satisfies three independently-argued positions, which is the strongest single architectural claim available here.

## 10. Adoption Inside the Organization

This is the section the rest of the design answers to, and it is also the section with the weakest evidence — a combination that must be handled by being explicit rather than confident.

**State the inferential distance first.** Of the sources bearing on adoption, none is about prompt asset registers, none is peer-reviewed, and none reports a controlled study or a sample size. They are operational diagnostics from marketing operations [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets], data governance consulting [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail], creative production operations [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog], a data-observability vendor arguing a category position [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery], and a data-catalog vendor's pitfall list [3-data-catalog-pitfalls-to-avoid-for-successful-governance]. The transfer from CRM/catalog/campaign-tracker adoption to prompt-register adoption is analogical judgment. What gives it weight is that four genuinely unrelated domains converge on the *same mechanism* without citing each other; what limits it is that convergence of diagnosis is not measurement of effect.

**The mechanism, named precisely.** "Teams revert to spreadsheets when the official system feels slower than the workaround, harder to trust, or disconnected from how the work actually happens" — and the first root cause listed is "The official platform adds more steps than it removes" [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. The same source supplies four more that map onto register design with uncomfortable directness. Tool-fit mismatch: "pipeline stages that do not reflect real sales movement... task structures that make handoffs harder instead of clearer" — which is exactly what a hand-maintained lifecycle enum becomes. Unclear ownership: "If no one governs fields, stages, handoffs, or data quality, the system degrades fast... The spreadsheet then feels safer because the team controls the logic directly." Reporting failure, with exports as the diagnostic tell: "Teams often export data into spreadsheets because the official reporting does not answer basic operational questions... Once reporting moves into spreadsheets, daily execution usually follows," and among the listed mistakes, "Treating reporting exports as normal instead of as a warning sign." And automation without a job: "Bad automation creates noise instead of leverage." That last one is a direct warning about the agent — an improvement agent that emits proposals nobody acts on is not a neutral feature; it is an active driver of reversion.

Turn the export tell into a product metric. **Export volume from the register's own browse view is a leading indicator of failure**, and should be instrumented on day one. If people export the prompt list to a Sheet in week one, the in-register view is not answering the question they actually have — which, for this population, is almost certainly "what is running in production right now and what is it costing us." That question must be answerable in one view without an export, and the demo should prove it.

**The migration rule, which constrains sequencing.** "The most common migration failure is asking a team to abandon a working tool before the replacement can do what the tool does," with a four-step remedy: replicate the three-to-five things the shadow spreadsheet does that the official system cannot; migrate live data, not just structure, because "Teams will abandon a new system the moment they need information that only exists in the old one"; assign a transition owner who validates coverage and flags gaps in the first four weeks; and enforce single source of truth from first live use — "if it is not in the system, it does not exist for decision-making purposes" [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog]. Step two is the whole argument for zero-labor ingestion in one sentence, and it is why the initial sweep must import content, not just structure.

**The incumbent's affordances, scored honestly.** The Google Sheet wins today on five things. The register matches two, approximates one, and deliberately declines one.

| Sheet affordance | Matched? | What compensates |
|---|---|---|
| **Instant edit** — click, type, done, no save dialog | **Approximated, not matched.** Structured fields are inherently more form-like than a blank cell. | The prompt body is one large freeform field with silent autosave and no submit button; every other field is optional or agent-inferred. Match on the field that matters, not on every field. |
| **No permission wall** — anyone with access just edits | **Deliberately not matched.** Mandatory HITL-to-promote is a hard constraint, not incidental friction. | Confine the wall to one action: promote-to-production. Drafting, editing, versioning and running evals stay as unwalled as a cell. Sheets never gated production releases, so nothing is being taken away. |
| **Free-text anything** — no schema, no required fields | **Partially matched.** A name/slug is required for API/SDK addressability. | Two required fields at creation (name, body). Everything else inferred from ingestion context, so structure exists without feeling imposed. |
| **Shareable link** — a URL opens the thing | **Fully matched. Required day one.** | SSO-scoped permalink to a specific prompt and version, deep-linked, no visible login flow for an already-authenticated member, no folder drilling. |
| **Zero learning curve** — years of muscle memory | **Cannot be matched by definition.** | Mimic the mental model instead of inventing one: default view is a sortable, filterable grid — rows are prompts, columns are owner, stage, cost, version — not a chat-first or graph-first interface. |

Two of five are structurally unmatchable and one is deliberately traded away. That means "replicate functionality before retiring the spreadsheet" cannot mean parity; it means **parity on the affordances that cause the abandonment decision — edit speed, unwalled authoring, link sharing — and an explicitly minimized footprint for the one affordance being traded, in exchange for the thing a Sheet structurally cannot do: versioned, auditable, cost-gated promotion.**

**Destination or discovery? Both, split by function.** One side of the argument holds that catalogs lose to discovery and that manual entry is the root failure [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery][why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. The other holds that a governed prompt requires a single source of truth with history, approvals and role-scoped access [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio][what-is-prompt-governance-the-critical-control-for-enterprise-ai], reinforced by serving mechanics where SDKs fetch a named prompt from a canonical store [caching-langfuse][guaranteed-availability-langfuse]. Both are right about different jobs, and the split is the design: **the ledger must be a destination, because unlike a data catalog it is not an index of assets held elsewhere — it *is* the asset of record. The capture surface must be embedded, because if the only way to add a prompt is to visit the register, the register will hold the prompts people remembered to file, which is precisely the population an asset ledger is useless for.** A destination-only build wins the demo and loses the six-month adoption war.

**The persona split, and the failure it predicts.** The catalog literature's expert-dependency outcome maps onto this product's three personas with unsettling precision: if the entry ticket is technical, the register becomes a developer tool, the developer becomes a bottleneck answering "which prompt should I use" questions, and the marketing and design teams keep pasting prompts into a chat thread — which is the status quo the problem statement already describes [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. Vendor content independently corroborates the shape of that status quo without being useful as evidence for the fix: "A subject matter expert tests prompt variations in a Google Sheet or a Jupyter Notebook... eventually share[s] a 'winning' prompt with the engineering team via Slack or email... Neither side has full context about what the other tested or why," with the summary that "There is no shared history of what was tried. There is no way to compare prompt versions systematically. There is no safe path from experiment to production" [prompt-management-for-non-engineers-how-product-teams-can-own-their-ai-prompts-a].

**Two boundary conditions that a Vietnamese conglomerate makes live.** First, the friction-differential mechanism dominates under *organic* adoption; a top-down mandate changes which axis dominates, and the same diagnostic treats "Leaders bypass the system" and "System use is optional" as a separate adoption-problem axis from tool fit [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. A conglomerate plausibly has mandate available, which reduces — but does not remove — the friction requirement, because a mandated system that is slower than the workaround produces parallel records rather than compliance. Second, and this is a genuine gap: **no source in this corpus addresses internal software-adoption behaviour inside a Vietnamese corporate group, the organizational hierarchy around approvals there, or whether Zalo rather than Slack is the real incumbent chat channel.** Every Vietnam source found is legal analysis. The embedded capture surface should therefore be built against whichever channel a five-minute internal survey identifies, and the demo should not assume Slack-shaped workflows transfer unmodified.

## 11. Build Sequence and Demo Proof Points

Sequence by what wins session one, then by what sustains week four, then by what survives year one. That ordering is not a compromise between adoption and governance — it follows from the finding that governance richness added before the friction win reads as "automation with no clear job" and actively drives reversion [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets].

**Phase 1 — the session-one surface (build first, build thin).**
1. One document-source connector plus a code scanner and a chat-export importer, producing raw candidate prompts with a recorded discovery method per record [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili].
2. Clustering: MinHash pre-filter plus embedding similarity at a high cosine threshold for the semantic pass [minhash-lsh-in-milvus-the-secret-weapon-for-fighting-duplicates-in-llm-training][retsim-resilient-and-efficient-text-similarity][understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices].
3. The grid: rows are prompts, columns are owner, stage, version, cost, last used. Sortable, filterable, permalinked.
4. Inline edit with silent autosave producing an automatic new version.
5. The `imported` state, visually distinct from governed records, with a one-click Claim.

**Phase 2 — the differentiation surface.**
6. The approval-decision object: pending state, evidence record, decision, reason, rejection path, immutable card snapshot. Build this early despite it being governance, because three independent lines of argument converge on it and no vendor ships it [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github][version-control-langfuse][release-labels-promptlayer][create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock].
7. Deterministic contract checks for one prompt family, with two or three named risk categories and per-category thresholds [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain].
8. One push adapter, with cache/TTL/async-refresh/hardcoded-fallback semantics reproduced [caching-langfuse][guaranteed-availability-langfuse][what-is-the-recommended-way-to-handle-remote-config-fetch-failures-unity-support].

**Phase 3 — the retention surface.**
9. OTel ingestion on non-content attributes; cost per prompt version; the ambient editor cost figure and the binding promote-time threshold [gen-ai-opentelemetry][llm-cost-attribution-at-scale-metadata-tagging-team-budgets-and-chargeback-repor].
10. Scheduled re-run of the contract against the live model — the drift monitor [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain][promptfoo-passes-production-still-breaks-heres-the-gap-dev-community].
11. RBAC tiers, with the contract-editing role separated from prompt editing [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms].
12. Embedded capture (browser action / forward-to-bot) against the group's actual chat channel.

**Stub or defer explicitly:** multi-provider adapters beyond the first; the improvement-proposal agent beyond a single scripted example; judge-based scoring of any kind; retirement automation; chargeback reporting. Every one of these is a standing maintenance liability, and the discipline is to leave them un-built until a prompt-register-specific need is demonstrated [paying-down-tech-debt-by-gergely-orosz-and-lou-franco][build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog].

**The demo, four beats, non-technical persona at the controls throughout.**

*Beat 1 — the ingestion beat, proving USP pillar 1 and inverting the catalog failure mode.* A real or seeded Sheet/Notion/chat export goes in; within the session the grid renders deduplicated and browsable. The visual payload is a cluster card: "7 near-identical versions of your welcome-email prompt found across 3 sources." Success criterion: the audience sees content they recognize, unprompted, with zero data entry, inside about a minute. If this beat fails, nothing later matters.

*Beat 2 — the edit beat, proving edit-speed parity.* Click into the prompt body, type, click away. A new version appears in history. No save button, no dialog, no approval. Then click Rollback and watch the production pointer move back in one action [version-control-langfuse].

*Beat 3 — the wall beat, proving the gate is exactly one moment.* Click Promote. One card appears: contract pass/fail by named category, blast radius (which products, how many calls), cost delta in VND per month, three divergent output pairs side by side, one agent-drafted purpose sentence with an edit affordance, and Approve / Reject-with-reason. Approve. Show the decision record — the card exactly as rendered, the reason, the actor, the timestamp — and say plainly that no observed vendor stores this object [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]. Then demonstrate the negative case: reject, and show the candidate returning to draft rather than vanishing.

*Beat 4 — the export-killer beat, proving the register answers the real question.* One view, no export: every prompt currently in production, its owner, its version, its 30-day cost, and its last-verified timestamp. This beat exists because the adoption diagnosis names in-system reporting failure as the mechanism by which daily execution migrates to a spreadsheet [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets], and it is the falsification test the design should be judged against: if users still export after seeing this view, ingestion alone was insufficient.

Add one beat *not* to perform: do not demo the agent rewriting a prompt and declaring it better. The evidence says that demo is both commoditized and unsound, and an audience member who knows the literature will ask what the score means [260122025-when-generic-prompt-improvements-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for][criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass].

## 12. Where the Framing Breaks — Risks and Counter-Evidence

**The evidence asymmetry, stated plainly.** The positive case rests on a founding premise that is asserted rather than measured for this setting; on adoption evidence transferred analogically from unrelated domains with no controlled study and nothing prompt-register-specific; and on a differentiating ingestion capability for which no source shows any competitor doing it — which is an argument for novelty, not for efficacy. The negative case rests on a widely-used model registry reversing its own lifecycle design after "extensive feedback" [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github]; on configuration-management field-ownership rot with a named mechanism [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both]; on data-catalog adoption failure among business users [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]; and on feature-store scepticism from a practitioner noting that "Other content on feature stores is often written by feature store providers" and that of the four canonical justifications, "'prevent repeated computation' and 'train-serve skew' do not apply to prompts at all" [you-still-dont-need-a-feature-store-xebia]. All of the negative sources are disinterested; none sells this product. A design document that presents the positive case with the confidence the negative case earns is lying.

**Risk 1 — the flagship differentiator manufactures the decay it exists to prevent. This is the keystone.** The two-tier field scheme works only for a register whose promote-approval genuinely touches every version that matters. But zero-labor bulk ingestion produces a register whose *majority* population has never passed through the one moment where meaning gets captured. On day 30 the register is large, browsable, and semantically empty — precisely the condition the catalog literature identifies as the onset of trust collapse, where automation "captures technical attributes... but it fails to answer critical business questions" [3-data-catalog-pitfalls-to-avoid-for-successful-governance]. This cannot be resolved by hand-waving about progressive enrichment.

**The position taken here: design it as an explicit two-population register.** Imported records land in an `imported` state that is visibly not part of the governed ledger — no owner badge, no production pointer, a provenance stamp reading "found in [source], not yet claimed," and a distinct visual treatment in the grid. The capture trigger for these records is **first use, not first promotion**: the moment a human opens, copies, edits or claims an imported record, one structured question fires ("what is this for?"), pre-drafted by the agent, one field, dismissible. This is defensible for three reasons. It preserves the sixty-second win, because import asks nothing. It prevents the false-ledger failure, because the ungoverned population cannot be mistaken for the governed one and the register's headline count is honest ("412 prompts discovered, 38 governed"). And it puts the question at the moment of demonstrated interest rather than at a moment of bulk indifference, which is the only point at which a human actually has an answer. The cost is that the ungoverned population may never shrink, and the register's own dashboard should say so: a governed-fraction metric, on the front page, as a self-diagnostic rather than a vanity number.

**Risk 2 — the promote gate is carrying four jobs and at most two fit in two minutes.** Section 6 resolves this by ordering and defaulting, and by demoting semantic capture to first-promotion-only. But the residual risk is real and unresolved by any source: nobody has measured whether approval-time confirm/edit fields get filled with real judgment or rubber-stamped. The rubber-stamp literature supplies failure signatures — approvals within minutes, substance-free comments, approving what was not read — but no quantified threshold [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]. **If approval-time confirmation turns out to be as neglected as a standalone stewardship task, the "collapse it into the promote step" fix does not solve the entry-ticket problem; it relocates it.** The mitigation is instrumentation rather than assertion: track edit rate on the pre-drafted purpose field. If it stays below a low threshold in a pilot, the two-tier scheme has failed and the honest response is to stop claiming the semantic fields are trustworthy, not to add a mandatory text box.

**Risk 3 — the gate does not scale.** The rubber-stamp mechanism is throughput-driven, so the DORA-survives-if-embedded reading holds at a conglomerate register's likely first-year volumes and would need revisiting at hundreds of promotions per week [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]. Design the escape now: per-prompt-family risk tiers, so low-blast-radius prompts can promote on contract-pass alone with post-hoc notification, reserving human approval for what the carve-out describes as decisions that genuinely require it.

**Risk 4 — vendor commoditization.** The register's differentiators are the cross-provider ledger, the approval object, and ingestion. A single-cloud vendor could close the first two within a release cycle for customers who standardize on one provider. The durable defence is not features; it is the ingestion corpus and the accumulated approval history, which are organization-specific and non-portable. This argues for treating the decision record as the product's data moat and designing it for export-resistance-by-value rather than lock-in.

**Risk 5 — the tool becomes real infrastructure without the team that implies.** A hackathon-origin platform that wins the internal adoption war "inherits exactly the multi-year maintenance burden... without the multi-year team" [paying-down-tech-debt-by-gergely-orosz-and-lou-franco]. The four-surface discipline is the mitigation, and it should be treated as a standing constraint rather than a phase-one convenience.

**Audited absences the report carries as findings.** (1) The full text of the textual-gradients critique was never retrieved; only its abstract is in evidence, and its mechanism claims are used at that level only [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat]. (2) **No disinterested source in the corpus argues against centralized prompt management on the merits.** Every negative voice — catalog failure, CMDB decay, feature-store scepticism, MLflow's own reversal, the change-approval literature — critiques an implementation, not the premise. The strongest available opposition is about *how*, not *whether*, and that absence should be reported rather than mistaken for endorsement. (3) No empirical measurement exists of non-technical business-user adoption of any AI-artifact register; the entire adoption argument is analogical. (4) No source studies judge validity on marketing, advertising or brand-voice copy; essay marking is a labelled proxy throughout. (5) No Vietnam- or conglomerate-specific evidence exists on organizational approval hierarchy or on which chat channel is the real incumbent. (6) The Evidently optimizer reports no measured train/test generalization gap [how-we-built-open-source-automated-prompt-optimization], and the vendor-relayed Gartner cancellation figure is not independently verified [servicenow-cmdb-best-practices-2026-guide-virima].

**Forward view, 12–24 months.** Three things change the calculus. First, vendor convergence: prompt storage, versioning and label-based rollback are already commodity, and the approval object is the kind of gap that gets closed when enough enterprise buyers ask — so the window in which "no vendor ships a pending-review/evidence-record/rejection-path object" is a differentiator is measured in quarters, not years, and the build sequence should spend it. Second, the regulatory clock: Vietnam's grace periods expire 1 March 2027 for most sectors and 1 September 2027 for health, education and finance [vietnam-artificial-intelligence-law-foundation-and-outlook-insight-baker-mckenzi][vietnam-the-first-law-on-artificial-intelligence-what-you-must-know-duane-morris], and the deployer obligations that then bite include documentation, activity-log retention and demonstrable human-intervention capacity [companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]. The register does not become legally required, but its approval record and its per-prompt provenance become the cheapest available evidence for products that do fall in scope — which is the moment the internal business case stops depending on adoption enthusiasm. Third, and most durable: drift is structural and getting worse as providers ship silent updates faster than deployers can test them, with the underlying asymmetry that hosted providers do not "define a behavioral specification that deployer can test against" [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. A register that runs the same deterministic contract against the live model on a schedule owns the only defensible answer to "is our production behaviour still what we approved?" That capability, not versioning and not the agent, is what the platform will be valued for in two years — and it is the one the adoption-first design gets almost for free, because the contract was already written to make the promote card honest.

## Source Tensions

**Fewer tokens versus lower cost.** One side reports compression up to 20x at under 2% quality loss and moderate compression delivering large cost reductions [prompt-compression-cut-token-costs-without-losing-quality-neuraltrust], with a formal treatment establishing that meaningful compression for black-box models is theoretically achievable [240715504-fundamental-limits-of-prompt-compression-a-rate-distortion-framework-f]. The other reports a 17% input-token cut raising total session cost 67% because the model paid to recover stripped meaning [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse]. Both are right about different measures, and the disagreement dissolves into a measurement rule. The narrow, well-supported claim — and the only one the design needs — is that **input-token delta is not a valid proxy for cost delta**. A gate that alerts on prompt length can fire on changes that reduce total spend and stay silent on changes that increase it, which is worse than no gate because it is confidently wrong. Measure cost per successful completed task over a traffic sample, retries included, attributed to the prompt version. Note the evidence balance honestly: one well-instrumented practitioner report against two sources including a formal analysis, so the claim is about the proxy, not about whether compression works.

**Judges that match humans versus judges that cannot rank at all.** The optimistic figure — over 80% agreement, "the same level of agreement between humans" [230605685-judging-llm-as-a-judge-with-mt-bench-and-chatbot-arena] — is not contradicted so much as *explained* by the pessimistic evidence. The variable is criterion-referenceability: where a task has a checkable target, judges reach ρ>0.6 to 0.885; where the target is holistic and human raters themselves reach only ρ=0.054, judge rank agreement is statistically indistinguishable from zero with every confidence interval spanning it [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass]. Best-effort debiasing caps at 71.0% (κ=0.549) even on natural preference data [judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm], and practitioners independently report that evaluation results resist translation into action [260416304-results-actionability-gap-understanding-how-practitioners-evaluate-llm]. The pessimistic side wins, decisively, and specifically for this product: **the eval gate is most trustworthy on the work this product handles least and least trustworthy on the work it handles most.** The consequence is not to abandon evaluation but to split it — block on deterministic contract, surface subjective quality as adjudicable examples rather than a score, and label the essay-to-marketing-copy analogy as the proxy it is.

**The register as destination versus discovery in the flow.** One side holds that a central catalogue people must visit is the wrong shape and that manual entry is the root failure [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery][why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. The other holds that a governed prompt store needs a canonical location with history, approvals and role-scoped access, reinforced by serving mechanics that fetch a named prompt from a canonical store [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio][what-is-prompt-governance-the-critical-control-for-enterprise-ai][caching-langfuse][guaranteed-availability-langfuse]. The word "catalogue" conflates two jobs. **The ledger is a destination — it is the asset of record, not an index of assets held elsewhere. The capture surface must be embedded where prompts are actually written.** The testable consequence: if the only way to add a prompt is to visit the register, the register holds the prompts people remembered to file, which is exactly the population an asset ledger is useless for.

**Approval as a drag on delivery versus approval as accountability.** The delivery-performance evidence is strong and specific: 2.6x more likely to be low performers with formal external approval, no correlation with change fail rate, "worse than having no change approval process at all," and regulator data showing CABs approving over 90% of major changes with some firms rejecting none in a year [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval][when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz][the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection][rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]. The accountability side notes that the same paper arguing review becomes theatre carves out "regulated code paths where a named human must bear legal accountability," and that record-keeping and lifecycle obligations attach to high-risk systems in both the EU and Vietnam [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act][companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]. The tension largely dissolves once you notice what was measured: approval by a body *external* to the change, reconstructing the reasoning itself. The prescribed remedy is embedded peer review captured in the platform — which is structurally what an accountable owner approving from a pre-computed card already is. Two honesty requirements survive: the theatre mechanism is throughput-driven and would need revisiting at hundreds of promotions per week; and **neither the EU AI Act as read nor Vietnam's Law and Decree obliges a pre-release human sign-off for an internal prompt-management layer, so the honest basis for mandatory HITL is segregation of duties plus prophylactic readiness, not a present statutory command.**

**What a prompt inventory can actually see.** Egress-boundary discovery captures traffic to known model endpoints and turns shadow into observed [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance]; source scanning extracts prompt literals before anything runs [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili]. **Neither can see the population this product exists to govern, and that is the finding.** Egress cannot attribute traffic to an authored, versioned prompt and sees nothing about a prompt sitting unused in a Notion page; source scanning is blind to Sheets, Drive, chat threads and shared accounts, which is where the problem statement says most of them live. The gap between the two methods *is* the document-and-chat estate. Document-source connectors are a structurally required third method, and the register must record which method found each record so coverage claims stay honest. Note also that the shadow-AI sources are substantially derivative of one another and recycle unattributed statistics; cite them for method, never for prevalence.

**Whether a prompt is an asset in any sense that survives scrutiny.** The pro-asset chorus is convergent but interested — trade press, consultancies and vendors [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio][what-is-prompt-governance-the-critical-control-for-enterprise-ai][centralized-prompt-management-for-enterprise-2026-framework][your-companys-most-valuable-asset-in-the-age-of-ai-is-walking-out-the-door]. The sceptical side is right on the formal test: recognition requires identifiability, control and probable future benefits, and internally generated know-how-type intangibles are barred because their cost cannot be separated from developing the business as a whole [recognition-and-cost-of-intangible-assets-ias-38-ifrs-community], while the only disinterested empirical measurement studies public repositories rather than corporate estates [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices] and the mature analogues show registers decaying [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both][why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. **The claim doing the work is managerial, not accounting.** "Sổ tài sản" earns its keep if and only if the register carries a named owner, a lifecycle with a retirement path, a record of what uses it, and a cost — and it should be judged on whether those four fields are populated and true, not on whether a prompt could appear on a balance sheet. Getting this wrong has a specific cost: an unearned asset claim invites the fair objection that this is a database with an expensive name.

**Git's branching versus the label pointer a non-developer can move.** Git-based versioning offers multi-object versioning, branching, parallel multi-environment workflows and straightforward revert [git-based-cms-vs-api-driven-cms-which-headless-cms-should-you-choose-bejamas][api-first-cms-vs-git-based]. A movable label lets a non-developer publish and roll back without a deploy, with the application picking up the change on its next fetch [version-control-langfuse][release-labels-promptlayer], and git-based approaches are noted as unsuitable where content changes rapidly [api-first-cms-vs-git-based]. **The label side follows necessarily from the fixed choice of the non-developer as primary user, so the architecture is settled — but the cost must be paid explicitly, not waved away.** Two people editing the same prompt in different directions produce colliding linear versions or, more likely, a second near-duplicate asset under a different name, and nothing in the versioning primitive catches that. Only the register's own duplicate clustering catches it, after the fact. That reframes clustering from an onboarding convenience into the standing compensating control for an architecture that cannot merge — which is why it must run continuously, not only at import.

## Opinionated Synthesis

Build a ledger that writes itself, put one wall in it, and let the marketer discover the governance by accident.

The concrete recommendation, ranked. **First, ingestion, because it is the only feature that can beat a bookmarked Sheet in sixty seconds.** Scan the code, connect one document source, import a chat export, cluster with embeddings rather than MinHash alone, and land everything in a visibly ungoverned `imported` state. The first session must show the user their own prompts, deduplicated, with no data entry — not because it is impressive but because manual population is the named mechanism by which every adjacent register in the evidence base failed [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery][why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail][why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. **Second, the approval-decision object**, because it is simultaneously the confirmed market gap across four vendors, the one governance surface worth building bespoke, and the natural output target for the agent — one component satisfying three independent arguments, with the confirmation running to database-schema granularity [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]. **Third, deterministic contract checks and cost attribution**, because together they are what makes the approval card decidable in two minutes without asking a non-engineer to interpret a statistic that carries no rank information. **Fourth and last, RBAC granularity and the improvement agent**, deferred not because they are unimportant but because introduced before the friction win they read as automation with no clear job, which is itself a documented driver of reversion [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets].

The hard constraints survive, and none of them is argued away. HITL survives as exactly one wall at promote-to-production, held by an accountable owner with context, fed by a pre-computed card, escapable by a logged break-glass path, and grounded in segregation of duties rather than a statutory claim that would not survive counsel review. RBAC survives, repointed: prompt text open to everyone, promotion restricted to the owner, eval-contract editing restricted more tightly still, because the contract is exactly as unstable and as consequential as the prompt it gates [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms]. Regression eval survives as three enforced parts rather than one dishonest verdict — block on contract, report subjective quality as adjudicable examples, monitor for drift on a schedule — and this report never claims the gate guarantees no quality degradation, because on this product's primary workload no gate can. Cost alerting survives, measured end-to-end per successful task rather than in prompt tokens, ambient while editing and binding at promotion.

The thing to be most honest about is also the thing to build first. The register's flagship differentiator manufactures the exact decay the register exists to prevent: bulk import produces a population that never passed through the one moment where meaning gets captured. The answer taken here is a two-population design with first *use* as the capture trigger, an honest governed-fraction metric on the front page, and instrumentation on the purpose-field edit rate so that if approval-time capture turns out to be rubber-stamped, the product finds out from its own telemetry rather than from a consultant three years later. That is the register's real test, and it arrives around day 30 — long after the demo, and long before anyone would otherwise notice.

Two claims deserve to be stated with different confidence levels, and the difference matters. The mechanism claims — how label-based promotion works, what the caching bar is, what OpenTelemetry standardizes, where judge validity collapses, what drift looks like — are well evidenced, in several cases across independent sources with no counter-evidence. The adoption claims are not. They are analogical transfers from marketing operations, data governance, creative operations and data observability, with no controlled study, no reported sample size, and nothing prompt-register-specific in the entire corpus. What makes the transfer worth acting on is that four unrelated domains name the same mechanism — the friction differential at the moment of use — without citing each other, and that the mechanism generates falsifiable predictions this build can test in week one: does anyone export the grid to a Sheet, does the purpose field get edited, does the median approval take two minutes or twelve seconds. Ship those three instruments alongside the product. A register that measures its own adoption failure modes is the only version of this design that improves after Demo Day, and it is the only honest way to hold a fixed positioning — "Sổ tài sản prompt của tập đoàn" — while admitting that the evidence for it is thinner than the evidence against it.
