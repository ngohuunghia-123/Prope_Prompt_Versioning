# Sổ tài sản prompt của tập đoàn — Building the Smallest Thing That Cannot Be Bought

**Answer first.** Build four things and inherit everything else. The four are: (1) a **cross-provider asset schema** — one record per prompt carrying owner, lifecycle state, current-production pointer, usage, cost and eval contract, normalized across Bedrock, Vertex and self-hosted runtimes; (2) an **approval-decision object** — a real state machine with a pending-review state, an attached evidence record, a named reviewer distinct from the actor, and a rejection path, rather than a role-gated write to a label; (3) **per-runtime push adapters** that sync the approved version down into whichever prompt store each product team already uses; and (4) **non-content telemetry ingestion** mapped onto OpenTelemetry's GenAI semantic conventions (an open standard defining vendor-neutral attribute names for LLM calls), so that usage and cost attach to a prompt version without the register ever touching prompt or completion content [gen-ai-opentelemetry]. Everything else the brief asks for — storage, immutable version numbering, diff views, playgrounds, label-as-pointer deployment, client caching — is commodity, already shipped, and already operated at a reliability standard a small team on a short timeline cannot match [version-control-langfuse] [caching-langfuse] [guaranteed-availability-langfuse] [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation].

Of those four, **the approval-decision object is the keystone**, and it is the single strongest architectural claim in this report. It is simultaneously three things at once. It is the confirmed market gap: across Langfuse, PromptLayer, Bedrock and Vertex, promotion is universally implemented as a role-gated write to a label, an alias or a version pointer — no pending-review state, no evidence record, no reviewer distinct from actor, no rejection path [version-control-langfuse] [release-labels-promptlayer] [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. Langfuse's own engineering documentation concedes the point in its own voice: as of July 2026 it "does not ship a built-in pull-request-style approval flow for prompt promotions," and lists "Multi-step approval workflow" in the **Composed** column rather than the **Native** column of its own capability table [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. It is the surface a disciplined build/buy analysis independently sanctions, because it is the one requirement that is genuinely bespoke rather than merely unbuilt [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. And it is the output target of the improvement agent: what the agent should hand a human is an evidence card, not a raw diff with a self-narrated rationale, because the causal stories auto-optimizers attach to their own edits are not reliable explanations of why the edit works [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat]. One component satisfying three independently-derived requirements is not a coincidence; it is the shape of the product.

This angle has an obvious failure mode and this report confronts it rather than hiding it: minimalism can shrink a product below the point of being worth adopting. The ingestion-and-clustering agent and the embedded capture surfaces — the two things that make this product distinctive against the real incumbent, which is a Google Sheet plus a Notion page plus a chat thread — sit *outside* the four sanctioned surfaces. The resolution is sequencing, not arbitration: the differentiators get built **first and thinly**, the four surfaces get built **durably**, and duplicate clustering earns permanent status not as onboarding sugar but as the standing compensating control for a versioning model that structurally cannot merge.

Two honesty commitments run through everything below. The eval gate cannot guarantee no quality degradation and must not be sold as if it could: **block on contract, report on quality, monitor for drift**. And the basis for mandatory human approval is ordinary corporate segregation of duties plus prophylactic readiness for the group's downstream products — not a present statutory command binding the register itself.

## 1. What the Asset Framing Commits You To — And the Prior Art It Inherits

### 1.1 The framing is a build constraint, not a slogan

"Sổ tài sản prompt của tập đoàn" is doing real architectural work, and it is worth being precise about what work. An asset register is not a content store with permissions bolted on. It is a record that carries, for every item, four things: a **named owner**, a **lifecycle with a retirement path**, a **record of what the item is used by**, and a **cost attached**. That is the entire test. If those four fields are populated and true at day ninety, the name is earned; if they are blank or lying, the product is a database with an expensive name.

This is a stricter commitment than it sounds, because it immediately rules out the cheapest implementation. A register whose owner field is a dropdown someone fills in at creation time will be wrong within a quarter; a register whose usage field requires a human to record where a prompt is deployed will be empty. The design consequence — which the rest of this report treats as binding — is that **every operational field must be derived from an action someone was already going to take**. Owner is derived from who promoted; the production pointer is derived from the promote action itself; usage is derived from SDK fetch calls and traces; cost is derived from telemetry. This is precisely the correction MLflow applied to its own model registry after years of complaints, replacing a fixed four-stage enum with reassignable aliases that decouple "what is in production" from a manually-maintained flag [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github].

### 1.2 The prior art you inherit — and the accounting limit, stated once

The strongest articulation of the governed-prompt idea in the record is practitioner-authored, not vendor-authored, and it names a bundle rather than a string: "Just as data governance requires a schema, prompt governance requires a definition of what 'good' looks like... Once designed, we lock the prompt together with the specific model version and configuration parameters... This bundle belongs in a shared repository, not a private folder or a DM" [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio]. That is a useful skeleton and this design adopts it. There is also a market-incentive reading worth carrying: every approval, override and correction a human makes "is producing a labeled training example that encodes judgment no public dataset contains" — which reframes the promote decision itself as the asset-generating event, not merely a control [your-companys-most-valuable-asset-in-the-age-of-ai-is-walking-out-the-door]. That is one columnist's argument rather than a study, and it should carry weight only as a design intuition.

The accounting objection deserves to be stated plainly, once, and then set aside. Formal intangible-asset recognition requires identifiability, probable future economic benefits, control over those benefits, and reliable cost measurement; internally generated know-how-type items — training activities, start-up activities, internally generated brands and "similar items" — are explicitly expensed "because such expenditure cannot be distinguished from expenditure to develop the business as a whole" [recognition-and-cost-of-intangible-assets-ias-38-ifrs-community]. A corporate prompt library resembles that excluded category far more than it resembles software or a licence. So: no prompt is going on a balance sheet, and nobody should pitch it as if one might. The claim doing the work is **managerial**, not accounting — and it is stronger for being narrowed, because a managerial claim can be falsified by inspecting four fields, whereas an accounting claim invites a reasonable objection nobody in the room can rebut.

### 1.3 The founding premise: honest about what was measured

The single disinterested empirical measurement of prompt duplication in the record studies GitHub repositories, and its figures are specific: internal exact duplication in 13.0% (12/92) of repositories, internal semantic duplication in 30.4% (28/92), external exact 16.3% (15/92), external semantic 34.8% (32/92), with prompt-level semantic duplication at 38.5% (9,553/24,800) [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices]. Two caveats matter. First, 72.8% of that corpus is public prompt-collection repositories whose entire purpose is aggregating popular prompts, so cross-repo duplication there plausibly reflects benign convergence rather than organizational waste — the internal figures (13.0%/30.4%) are the defensible ones. Second, and decisively for this product: that population has git. It has version control, code review, searchable history and diffs, **and it still shows 30.4% internal semantic duplication**.

The inference this licenses is *a fortiori* and should be labelled as such: no source measures duplication inside a single organization's Sheets/Notion/Drive/chat estate — that is an audited absence — but a population with none of git's affordances should not duplicate less than one that has them all [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices]. That reasoning gets the founding premise where it needs to go without pretending the measurement was taken.

## 2. The Asset Record — Prompt Identity, Data Model, and Lifecycle

### 2.1 The bespoke surface is the schema, not the storage

Here is the cleanest way to see what is actually missing from the market. Bedrock's prompt management creates a prompt inside a single AWS account, behind "an IAM identity that has permissions to use the Amazon Bedrock console," returns "a `DRAFT` version and... an ID and ARN," and retrieves by flat numbered version through `GetPrompt` [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [view-information-about-versions-of-a-prompt-in-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock]. Vertex is explicit where Bedrock is merely implicit: its capabilities are for managing prompts "**within your Google Cloud project**," the client is instantiated as `vertexai.Client(project='my-project', location='my-location')`, and `list()` returns references "for all saved prompts **in the current project**" [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. Even Langfuse, which ships the richest environment-label model in the record, concedes that its own prompts "are scoped to a project — if you use separate projects for different environments, see how to sync prompts between them" [version-control-langfuse].

Chain the two vendor mechanisms together and the conclusion follows without any vendor asserting it: **a conglomerate running products on Bedrock and Vertex simultaneously cannot purchase a single ledger, because neither product's scope unit spans the other's.** That is an inference from two mechanism documents, stated as an inference; neither vendor makes a comparative claim and neither is being cited for one. Buying a larger Bedrock plan does not produce a Vertex-side view of a Bedrock prompt. Honesty requires one more line: cross-account sharing within a single cloud — AWS Resource Access Manager, Vertex's organization and folder hierarchy — was not verified against prompt resources in the sources read here. That is an audited absence, and it means the claim holds firmly *across* providers while remaining unverified *within* one provider's multi-account estate. Even in the most generous reading, an intra-AWS sharing primitive would not span Bedrock and Vertex, which is the case a mixed-provider group actually faces.

### 2.2 The two-tier field model

The schema splits into two tiers with different capture physics.

**Tier 1 — operational fields, 100% auto-derived, no exceptions.** Owner (from the promote actor), current-production version (from the promote action, in MLflow's reassignable-alias shape rather than a manually-set stage flag [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github]), consuming products and environments (from SDK fetch calls and adapter pushes), usage volume and cost (from telemetry, per §7), last-used timestamp, runtime provider (`gen_ai.provider.name`, whose documented enum includes `aws.bedrock`, `gcp.vertex_ai`, `openai`, `anthropic`), and model identity (`gen_ai.request.model` / `gen_ai.response.model`) [gen-ai-opentelemetry].

**Tier 2 — semantic fields, which cannot be auto-derived.** Purpose, canonicalization judgment ("this cluster of eleven near-identical prompts is really one asset"), deprecation rationale, criticality class. These decay under any separate-stewardship model and survive only if their capture is folded into a step the organization already mandates — which, given the hard HITL constraint, means the promote-approval step.

### 2.3 Provenance per field, not per record

One design rule transfers directly from mature configuration-management practice and is worth more than it costs: **stamp every field with the method that populated it and when, not just a bare updated-at timestamp.** The failure mode is named precisely — "'Last updated' timestamps on CI records are often artifacts of a bulk import or administrative edit, not evidence of a verified... scan," while a mature practice "tags every CI attribute with its discovery source and scan timestamp, giving agents and auditors a clear confidence signal for each value" [servicenow-cmdb-best-practices-2026-guide-virima]. Applied here: a prompt whose owner field came from a bulk Notion import should render differently from one whose owner field came from a promote click. This is what lets an approver, an auditor, or the agent itself treat a record's confidence as a first-class property. It is also the mechanism that makes the coverage claims in §3 honest.

The second transferable rule is the one-page ownership agreement. The most common finding in decayed registers is not missing data but contested data — "two systems exist, but neither one is being trusted, because they disagree on the basics — and no one has decided which one wins on which field. The fix is rarely a new tool. It's a one-page 'who owns which column' agreement" [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both]. In a push architecture this is not optional. The register says v5 is production; a developer with console access edits the Bedrock prompt directly. Somebody must have decided, in advance and in writing, which one wins on that field. This report's answer: **the register wins on lifecycle, ownership and approval; the runtime wins on nothing.** Direct console edits must be detected by the adapter's reconciliation pass and surfaced as drift, not silently absorbed.

### 2.4 The retirement path

An asset register without a retirement path is an inventory. The lifecycle states this design needs are: `discovered` (found by ingestion, unclaimed), `draft`, `pending-review`, `production` (per environment, per consuming product), `superseded`, and `retired`. The one to argue for is `discovered` as a genuinely distinct state rather than a tag — for reasons developed at length in §3 and §12, because it is the mechanism that keeps the mass-import population from silently contaminating the governed population.

## 3. Ingestion: Starting From the Existing Chaos

### 3.1 Three methods, and only one of them sees the actual problem

The security industry has already built two prompt-discovery patterns and neither one sees the population this product exists to govern. Static analysis parses source files and extracts the string literals passed to LLM APIs, producing a structured inventory before any prompt reaches a model — a "Prompt SBOM," "a CycloneDX v1.4 structured inventory of every prompt string in the codebase... The string content and its hash, the source file and line number," built from AST parsing across TypeScript/JS/Python/Go/Rust/Java/C# [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili]. That same source names its own blind spot: "Prompts assembled at runtime from multiple variables, database values, or user inputs are not visible to static analysis by definition," and "Static analysis does not replace runtime screening. The two layers are complementary by design" [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili]. The other pattern, egress-boundary discovery, observes traffic to known LLM domains but cannot attribute a call to an authored, versioned prompt, and sees nothing at all about a prompt sitting unused in a Notion page [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance].

The finding is the gap between them. By the problem's own account, most of the group's prompts live in Sheets, Notion, Drive, chat threads and shared accounts. Neither method reaches there. **Document-source connectors are therefore a structurally required third method, not a nice-to-have**, and the register must record which discovery method found each prompt so that coverage claims stay honest [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili] [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance]. This is the per-field provenance rule from §2.3 doing its first job.

A note on sourcing discipline: the shadow-AI material in this record is substantially derivative — several pieces recycle each other's unattributed prevalence statistics — so it is used here for method and framing only, never for numbers [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance].

### 3.2 Clustering: the cheap default is the wrong default

Near-duplicate detection at scale is a solved engineering problem with a well-known cheap answer, and the cheap answer is wrong for this population. MinHash locality-sensitive hashing is genuinely industrial — one production deployment "deduplicate[d] 10 billion documents efficiently... more than doubled processing speed and achieved 3-5x cost savings" against a MapReduce baseline [minhash-lsh-in-milvus-the-secret-weapon-for-fighting-duplicates-in-llm-training]. But peer-reviewed benchmarking on adversarial and typo-laden short text — which is exactly the profile of a prompt pasted into Zalo, edited by hand, pasted again into a Sheet — reports MinHash at Recall@1 of 0.538 averaged across 41 languages against a neural near-duplicate method at 0.977 (English: 0.591 versus 0.987), and on real-world near-duplicate detection the neural method "outperforms all other approaches by a significant margin (4.8% ARI compared to our best MinHash result)" [retsim-resilient-and-efficient-text-similarity].

The design call: **cluster with embeddings, not hashes.** The GitHub study itself used cosine similarity above 0.95 on sentence embeddings to find its semantic duplicates [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices], and the neural approach's reported cost is real — roughly 46x slower than MinHash on CPU, competitive only on GPU [retsim-resilient-and-efficient-text-similarity]. For a corporate estate measured in thousands of prompts rather than billions of documents, that premium is trivially affordable, and paying it is the difference between clustering that finds the eleven variants of the same product-description prompt and clustering that finds three of them.

### 3.3 Ingestion is where the entry ticket gets paid or dodged

The mechanism that kills adjacent catalog products is named consistently: the "entry ticket" is too high, so "the catalogue is gradually becoming a tool reserved for data teams" while "the departments... continue to operate outside the system," producing expert dependency measured in "several hours a week simply answering comprehension questions or manually validating metadata" [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. Independently, from a different vendor category: traditional catalogs "rely on data teams to do the heavy lifting of manual data entry... time-intensive, but requires significant manual toil that could otherwise be automated" [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery]. If the ingestion and clustering interface requires technical fluency to operate — if a marketer must understand what a similarity threshold is to accept a merge — the product inverts its own persona bet and becomes exactly what these sources predict.

The interface consequence is concrete: clustering decisions must be presented as "these five look like the same prompt — keep which one?" with a one-click answer and a visible undo, never as a threshold slider. And the agent's tools for this must be built to the discipline a primary engineering source names: do not "merely wrap existing software functionality or API endpoints," and implement "pagination, range selection, filtering, and/or truncation with sensible default parameter values for any tool responses that could use up lots of context" [writing-effective-tools-for-ai-agentsusing-ai-agents-anthropic].

## 4. Versioning, Release, and Rollback Mechanics

### 4.1 Inherit the primitive; it is free and it is better than what you would build

Label-based versioning is the correct substrate and it should be inherited rather than reimplemented. The mechanism is simple and well-documented: "Each prompt version is automatically assigned a version ID. Additionally, you can assign labels to follow your own versioning scheme"; "To 'deploy' a prompt version, you have to assign the label `production`... to that prompt version"; and "You can quickly rollback to a previous version by setting the `production` label to that previous version" [version-control-langfuse]. PromptLayer's release labels are mechanically identical — "hover over a prompt template version, click 'Add Release Label'" [release-labels-promptlayer]. Bedrock snapshots immutable numbered versions from a `DRAFT` [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock]; Vertex offers `create_version`, `get_version`, `list_versions` and `restore_version` [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation].

This is the same decoupling of deployment from release that feature-flag practice formalized a decade ago: separate "the process of deploying code to a production environment from that of releasing the code (feature) to end users," which "improves stability by controlling timing and having instant remediation," and — critically for a product whose primary user is a marketer — "enabl[es] organizations to expand who can play a role in the release process," including "product managers, operations teams, or whoever should have the final say" [the-importance-of-decoupling-deployment-release-launchdarkly]. A movable pointer is what lets a non-developer publish and roll back without a deploy. That is the operative requirement, and it settles the architecture.

### 4.2 The uncompensated cost, named rather than waved away

The countervailing case is real and must be paid explicitly. Git-based content workflows offer multi-object versioning, branching, parallel multi-environment workflows and straightforward reverts; database-backed content stores are described as not supporting "sophisticated versioning, distributed and parallel multi-environment workflows, or branching," all of which are ordinary enterprise content requirements [git-based-cms-vs-api-driven-cms-which-headless-cms-should-you-choose-bejamas] [api-first-cms-vs-git-based]. Label-based versioning gives you a **linear history with movable environment pointers, not divergent branches with reconciliation.**

Follow that through to the operational consequence, which is the part usually skipped. Two people editing the same prompt in different directions produce either colliding linear versions or — far more likely in an organization where the incumbent tool is a Sheet — a second near-duplicate asset under a different name. **Nothing in the versioning primitive catches that.** Only the register's own duplicate clustering catches it, and only after the fact. This converts §3.2 from an onboarding convenience into **the standing compensating control for an architecture that cannot merge**, and it is the reason clustering must run continuously against the live register rather than once at migration. Framing it this way turns an admitted weakness into the argument for the product's own differentiator without pretending the weakness is not there.

### 4.3 The register's own schema needs a compatibility contract

One inherited discipline is underrated and cheap: the register will expose an asset schema to downstream SDK consumers, and that schema will change. Schema-registry practice supplies the taxonomy off the shelf — "Backward - consumers using new schema can read data written with old schema (add optional fields, remove fields). Forward - consumers using old schema can read data written with new schema... Full - both backward and forward compatible," with BACKWARD as the default mode [schema-evolution-compatibility-types-backward-forward-full-transitive-confluent]. Declare BACKWARD compatibility for the served-prompt payload on day one and enforce it in CI. The alternative — an implicit contract — means every schema change is a coordinated release across every product team in the group, which is precisely the coupling the label pointer was adopted to avoid.

### 4.4 What "rollback" actually costs in wall-clock time

Rollback is not instantaneous and the design should say so. Client SDKs cache prompts with a default TTL of 60 seconds and revalidate in the background, so "running instances serve the rolled-back version within roughly one TTL of the label change, on their next fetch," with the TTL configurable per fetch [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse] [caching-langfuse]. That is a real and defensible number — faster than any code-deploy rollback — and the register's UI should show it, because an approver's blast-radius estimate is wrong if it assumes instant reversion.

## 5. Regression Eval You Can Actually Gate On

### 5.1 The constraint as written cannot be honoured, and the honest version is better

"Eval hồi quy để đảm bảo không giảm chất lượng" — regression eval to guarantee no quality degradation — cannot be implemented as a single automated verdict, and building something that claims to be one would be a fabricated guarantee dressed as governance. Three independent lines of evidence force this.

**Judge validity is a property of the task, not the model.** In a controlled multi-format study, structured questions reached fractional mean absolute errors around 0.22 with "robust discriminative validity (Spearman ρ>0.6)," rising to committee ρ=0.885 when official solutions were supplied, and rubric-constrained scientific plots reached ρ>0.84. But on holistic 0–100 essay marking, "blind AI marking is harsher and more variable than human marking, with discriminative validity already poor (ρ≈0.1)," and "Adding a mark scheme does not improve discrimination (ρ≈0; all confidence intervals include zero)" — committee values of 0.088 blind, −0.044 with scheme, 0.034 with scheme-plus-anchor [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass]. The human baseline explains why: human–human MAE of 6.84 marks and "average pairwise Spearman correlation of just ρ=0.054" — expert humans themselves cannot agree on rank ordering for that task class. The paper's own deployment diagnostic is the rule to adopt: "Before asking whether LLM-as-a-judge can be used to mark a task, one should first establish whether the task is sufficiently criterion-referenceable that human markers themselves assign stable and discriminative judgements" [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass].

The strongest opposing figure deserves its full force: GPT-4-as-judge was reported to "match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans" [230605685-judging-llm-as-a-judge-with-mt-bench-and-chatbot-arena]. But a more recent, adversarial-aware study applying nine debiasing strategies across five judges does not reproduce that as a floor: the best configuration of any tested reached 71.0% agreement (κ=0.549) at about $0.001 per evaluation, and the most common debiasing technique — position swap — "consistently and significantly hurts all models (−3 to −13 pp)" on adversarial instances [judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm]. Style bias, not position bias, is the dominant residual: four of five models show strong markdown preference (+0.40 to +0.76) while humans prefer markdown only 57% of the time versus 73–97% for the judges [judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm].

The analogy limit must be stated: **no source studies judge validity on marketing, advertising or brand-voice copy specifically.** Essay marking is the closest available proxy and is labelled as a proxy every time it is used here. That is an audited absence, not a resolved question.

**Aggregate scores mask category collapse.** A documented practitioner case: version 4 "improved overall accuracy by 10% while negation-handling collapsed 66.7%, from 100.0%→33.3%" — invisible to any single scalar eval delta [prompt-engineering-fails-quietly-prompt-regression-is-why-towards-data-science]. The same source supplies the framing this design adopts wholesale: "regression testing isn't really a quality problem — it's a contract problem... Checking if the output intent matches the expected intent is binary, so a judge model just adds noise."

**Drift is structurally invisible to any pre-release gate.** Chen, Zaharia and Zou (2024, *Harvard Data Science Review*) documented GPT-4's prime-number identification accuracy falling from 84% to 51% between March and June 2023, and code-execution success from 52% to 10% in the same window, with no version change [prompt-drift-what-it-is-and-how-to-detect-it-agenta-blog] [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. Corroborating incident: Anthropic's August 2025 postmortem "documents three bugs affecting Claude Sonnet 4's performance without any API modifications, including routing errors affecting up to 16% of requests" [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. A first-person practitioner account confirms the failure shape in a different toolchain: evals green in CI, then a silent model change 21 days later breaking JSON extraction on roughly 15% of calls with zero CI trigger — "PromptFoo catches regressions at step 1. It doesn't catch steps 2-6... Neither does the other's job" [promptfoo-passes-production-still-breaks-heres-the-gap-dev-community].

### 5.2 Block on contract, report on quality, monitor for drift

The implementable design is three enforced parts:

**Block** automatically and only on deterministic, code-checkable production-contract properties: schema conformance, required disclaimers, banned-phrase lists, length bounds, latency, and the cost measure defined in §7. The structure is risk-category-based, with per-category thresholds rather than one global score — worked examples give a backend SQL category "a pass rate of more than 95%," a JSON category that "requires 100% format compliance," and a security category that "requires 0% security violations" [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain].

**Report** LLM-judge quality scores as one advisory signal on the approver's evidence card, never as a verdict. This is where the eval literature stops being an academic caveat and becomes the justification for the HITL constraint: on the holistic copy this product's primary users produce, automated judgment carries near-zero rank information, so the human is the only mechanism in the pipeline capable of the judgment automation cannot supply [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass].

**Monitor** by re-running the same deterministic contract on a schedule against the currently-serving model, turning the compatibility gate into a recurring job rather than a promotion-time checkpoint [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. This is what makes any "no degradation" language defensible at all, and even then only for the properties the contract can check.

### 5.3 The permission boundary belongs around the contract

One architectural consequence is usually missed. Eval criteria are not stable: "developers' perceptions of what constitutes 'good' and 'bad' outputs shift as they interact with more data (i.e., criteria drift)... these predefined criteria are often incomplete" [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms]. Judge alignment is achievable but is maintained work — one documented trajectory improved human–judge agreement "from 68% to 94%" over three review iterations [what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms] — and "model-based evaluation is a meta-problem within your larger problem. You must maintain a mini-evaluation system to track its quality" [your-ai-product-needs-evals-hamels-blog].

Therefore: **prompt-text editing stays open to marketers and designers; edit rights over the production contract — thresholds and the critical-category list — sit behind a stricter, reviewed role.** A permission model that gates prompt text but leaves thresholds editable by the same person collapses the two-key structure HITL exists to provide. This is inference from the criteria-drift and eval-bottleneck findings rather than an observed governance case study; no source in this record studies prompt-registry permission design directly.

One further architectural hazard follows from a mechanistic finding: self-preference bias tracks output perplexity and familiarity rather than a detectable "this is mine" signal, so it is not prompt-fixable [241021819-self-preference-bias-in-llm-as-a-judge]. An agent that both proposes prompt edits and judges the resulting outputs is structurally exposed if generator and judge share a model family. **Decouple them by policy in the schema**: the eval contract records which model family judges, and the register refuses to run a judge from the same family as the proposer.

## 6. The Promote Gate — HITL, RBAC, and the Two-Minute Decision

### 6.1 Give the opposition its full force

The strongest evidence against a mandatory approval gate is unambiguous and should be quoted before it is reframed. "No evidence was found to support the hypothesis that a more formal, external review process was associated with lower change fail rates" [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval]. "Organizations with formal external approval processes were found to be 2.6 times more likely to be low performers," and, quoting *Accelerate* directly: "We found that external approvals were negatively correlated with lead time, deployment frequency, and restore time, and had no correlation with change fail rate... It is, in fact, worse than having no change approval process at all" [when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz]. The empirical signature of theatre is in the same source: across over a million production changes analysed by the UK FCA, "CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, and in some firms the CAB had not rejected a single change during 2019" [when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz].

A separate mechanism, throughput rather than formality, points the same way: as agent-driven change volume rises, "reviews of agent-generated code become rubber-stamps: the human approves because the code looks correct, because the tests pass, and because the cognitive cost of genuine scrutiny is prohibitive," against a baseline where developers at large organisations "spend between ten and fifteen percent of their working hours reading and commenting on others' code" [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]. The qualitative warning signs are documented — "PRs approved within minutes of being opened," "Reviewers frequently approving code they have not read" — but that source supplies **no quantified size threshold**; any "400 lines" or "1000 lines" figure attributed to it would be fabricated [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware].

### 6.2 What the evidence actually licenses

Read precisely, the DORA finding attaches to approval that is **external, formal and decontextualized** — a body reconstructing reasoning it was not present for. Its own prescribed remedy is not removal: "Use peer review to meet the goal of segregation of duties, with reviews, comments, and approvals captured in the team's development platform as part of the development process" [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval]. An accountable owner approving from a pre-computed evidence card, inside the platform, *is* that prescription — not a CAB in miniature. And the throughput paper's own carve-out describes production-prompt promotion better than it describes a routine commit: human approval "is reserved for the decisions that genuinely require it: high-risk changes, novel architecture choices, and regulated code paths where a named human must bear legal accountability" [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection].

Two honesty conditions attach. The scale caveat is real: the theatre mechanism is throughput-driven, so this reasoning holds at a corporate register's likely first-year volumes and would need revisiting at hundreds of promotions per week [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]. And the legal basis must be stated correctly, because getting it wrong would not survive counsel review.

### 6.3 The legal position, stated correctly

Neither regime obliges a pre-release human sign-off on an internal prompt-management layer. The EU's record-keeping article mandates that "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system" for post-market traceability — a logging obligation on the deployed system's operation, not an upstream approval gate [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act]. Vietnam's Article 14 deployer obligations for high-risk systems require the deployer to "operate and monitor the system in accordance with its intended purpose," to "ensure safety, data security and maintain the possibility of human intervention during use," and to meet transparency, incident-handling and accountability duties — a runtime intervention capacity, not a release-approval gate [companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]. The Decree's "internal use" exemption applies narrowly to AI-generated-content and deepfake labelling, not as a general carve-out from risk classification or documentation duties [update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi].

The timeline has **two distinct clocks and both are real**. The Law takes effect 1 March 2026 with grace periods for existing providers and deployers "lasting until 1 March 2027 (or 1 September 2027 for AI systems in the fields of health, education and finance)" — a 12-month general and 18-month sectoral window [vietnam-artificial-intelligence-law-foundation-and-outlook-insight-baker-mckenzi], independently corroborated as "18-Month Grace Period: For AI systems deployed in the fields of finance, healthcare, and education... 12-Month Grace Period: For AI systems operating in all other sectors" [vietnam-the-first-law-on-artificial-intelligence-what-you-must-know-duane-morris]. Separately, the Decree imposes a notification deadline: for any AI system operating before 1 March 2026, "Within 60 days from the effective date of the Decree (i.e., before June 30, 2026), transitioning organizations and enterprises must submit a notice on the one-stop portal along with a transition plan or compliance scheme" [update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi]. These are two obligations on two clocks; conflating them, or dropping either, misstates the position.

**The honest basis for mandatory HITL is therefore ordinary corporate segregation of duties, plus prophylactic readiness for the group's downstream products becoming regulated AI systems** — not a present statutory command on the register itself. That basis is more durable than a legal claim, because it does not evaporate when counsel reads the statute. And it has a concrete commercial payoff: the approval record the register produces is exactly the kind of artifact a downstream product team will need when it must evidence human oversight and documentation for its own classified system.

### 6.4 What the approval-decision object actually is

Here is the schema, because this is the keystone and it should be specified rather than gestured at.

An `ApprovalDecision` is a first-class row, not a label mutation. It carries: the target prompt and candidate version; the incumbent production version; a **state** in {`pending`, `approved`, `rejected`, `withdrawn`, `expired`}; the **requesting actor** and, separately, the **deciding reviewer**, with a hard constraint that they differ; a **frozen evidence record** (contract-check results per risk category, advisory quality scores with the judge model and version named, cost-per-successful-task delta, blast radius as the list of consuming products and their traffic share, and the consequence-language diff); a **decision rationale** field, required on rejection and optional on approval; a **semantic-capture payload** (§2.2's Tier 2 fields, AI-drafted and confirm-or-edit); and an immutable timestamp chain. Approval — and only approval — emits the pointer move to every runtime adapter.

Note what this buys that a protected label cannot. A protected label prevents the wrong person from writing; it does not record that a review happened, what was reviewed, what the reviewer saw, or that a proposal was rejected and why. Langfuse's own documentation describes the composition path honestly: protect the label, post CI evidence to a pull request, notify approvers by webhook, and keep audit logs on Enterprise plans — four separate mechanisms glued together to approximate one object [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. And the depth of the gap is confirmed at implementation granularity: for well over a year, moving a Langfuse label programmatically required raw SQL against a Postgres `prompts` table (`UPDATE prompts SET labels = ...`), with API support confirmed by a maintainer only as late as January 2025 [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]. There was never an approval object — only a labels column.

### 6.5 The overload problem, and what degrades gracefully

The gate is being asked to carry four jobs: read an eval signal, exercise judgment automation cannot supply, confirm AI-drafted semantic metadata, and check cost and blast radius — in two minutes. These are not obviously compatible, and asserting the card is well-designed would be evasion. The resolution is ordering and defaulting:

1. **Load-bearing, blocks, cannot be defaulted:** the contract-check result. If a risk category fails its threshold, the approve button is disabled, not merely red. The reviewer's judgment is not invited on schema conformance.
2. **Load-bearing, requires human judgment, cannot be pre-filled:** the consequence-language diff — "this change will make the assistant answer refund questions without mentioning the 14-day window" — plus the blast radius. This is the two minutes.
3. **Pre-filled with a safe default, overridable:** the advisory quality score and the cost delta, each rendered as a single line with a one-tap "why?" expansion. The default is "accept the signal as shown."
4. **Degrades first, and this should be admitted:** semantic capture. Under load, confirm-or-edit fields get rubber-stamped, and no source in this record resolves empirically whether promote-time confirmation survives better than a standalone stewardship task [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]. The fallback is a scope cut, not a hope: **capture semantic fields only on the first promotion of a new prompt asset, and on canonicalization merges — never on every version.** A version-level promote of an existing asset asks for nothing but the decision.

If the reviewer does nothing but click approve, the record still gains: a named accountable decider, a frozen evidence snapshot, a timestamp, a derived owner, and a derived production pointer. That is the design floor, and it is deliberately above zero.

## 7. Cost and Token Economics as a Release Control

### 7.1 The naive gate is worse than no gate

"Cảnh báo thay đổi làm tăng token/chi phí" reads, naively, as an alert on prompt-token delta. That gate would be confidently wrong in both directions and should not be built.

The compression-positive case deserves its strongest form. Vendor-relayed benchmark figures report compression "up to 20x with under 2% quality loss," a "17.1% performance improvement at 4x compression on long-context tasks," and selective-context methods achieving "50% context reduction while maintaining comparable performance: only 0.023 degradation in BERTScore" [prompt-compression-cut-token-costs-without-losing-quality-neuraltrust]. A formal treatment establishes that the compression/fidelity frontier is real and computable — the distortion-rate function derived "as a linear program," with "a large gap between the performance of current prompt compression methods and the optimal strategy" [240715504-fundamental-limits-of-prompt-compression-a-rate-distortion-framework-f]. Note the sourcing: the first is marketing content from a security vendor relaying research figures it did not produce, and the second was read at abstract level only.

Against it, a well-instrumented practitioner counter-example: "aggressive compression reduced input tokens by 17 percent and still increased total session cost by 67 percent. The model had less text to read and more work to do... Now it pays for that recovery with extra reasoning, extra retries, and extra correction loops" — the "compression tax" [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse].

These do not contradict each other; they measure different things. The claim to make is narrow and fully supported: **input-token delta is not a valid proxy for cost delta.** A gate that fires on it will alert on changes that reduce total spend and stay silent on changes that increase it. That is worse than no gate, because it is confidently wrong and will be trusted.

### 7.2 The measure that works, and it is buildable

Measure **cost per successfully completed task over a traffic sample, including retries and multi-turn recovery, attributed to the prompt version.** That sounds expensive and is not, because the whole thing composes from an open standard plus arithmetic.

The join key exists: `gen_ai.prompt.name` — "The name of the prompt that uniquely identifies it" — with usage attributes `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.output_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.cache_creation.input_tokens` and `gen_ai.usage.cache_read.input_tokens`, and provider/model identity via `gen_ai.provider.name`, `gen_ai.request.model` and `gen_ai.response.model` [gen-ai-opentelemetry]. Content attributes are separately gated and deprecated: `gen_ai.prompt` is "Removed, no replacement at this time," and `gen_ai.input.messages`/`gen_ai.output.messages` each carry "This attribute is likely to contain sensitive information including user/PII data," with instrumentations told they "MAY provide a way for users to filter or truncate" them [gen-ai-opentelemetry].

The inference this supports, stated as an inference from that single specification: **the cost-alert hard constraint can be satisfied entirely on non-content telemetry, so the register never needs to require prompt or completion content capture to compute the number the brief asks for.** For a conglomerate whose products handle customer data, that is not a footnote — it is what makes group-wide telemetry ingestion politically possible at all.

The architecture is off the shelf from LLM-gateway practice: per-request metadata tagging where a JSON metadata header is set by the application and stored on the trace with "a curated subset to low-cardinality metric labels"; per-trace cost "computed at span close from `gen_ai.usage` attributes and a versioned pricing table"; and two-tier enforcement with "soft alerts at 80% via PagerDuty or Slack, and hard limits that return HTTP 429 with a `budget_exhausted` error" [llm-cost-attribution-at-scale-metadata-tagging-team-budgets-and-chargeback-repor]. One arithmetic trap is flagged explicitly and must be reproduced correctly: "gen_ai.usage.input_tokens is defined as the total including both cache lines, so subtracting once for cache_read but not for cache_write... double-counts the cache-write portion" [llm-cost-attribution-at-scale-metadata-tagging-team-budgets-and-chargeback-repor]. A register that gets cache accounting wrong will report cost deltas that are wrong in the direction of looking good, which is the worst available error.

### 7.3 Cost as a gate, not a chart

The design commitment is that cost is **binding on the promote decision**, not a retrospective dashboard. Concretely: the candidate runs against a traffic sample; the register computes cost per successful completed task for candidate and incumbent; the delta appears on the evidence card as one line with a threshold; and exceeding the threshold moves the decision from "approve" to "approve with an explicit cost acknowledgement recorded in the decision rationale." Note this is a *contract* property in the §5.2 sense — deterministic, code-checkable, reproducible — which is why it can block, while quality cannot. That asymmetry is not a compromise; it is the only place in the brief where the "guarantee" language is actually honest.

## 8. Distribution to Products — API/SDK, Parity, and Failure Modes

### 8.1 The reliability bar you must inherit

The register will sit near, or in, the inference path of every downstream product in the group. The bar for that position is documented precisely and is non-trivial: prompts "cached client-side in the SDKs, so there's no latency impact after the first use and no availability risk"; stale-while-revalidate semantics where "stale prompts are served immediately while it revalidates in the background"; and a layered server cache where "The API caches prompts in a Redis cache... if Redis is unavailable, the database serves as backup" [caching-langfuse]. Even with all of that, a residual failure mode is named honestly: the fetch "will throw an exception if: No local (fresh or stale) cached prompt is available... **and** network request fails... after retries," with two mitigations — pre-fetch-and-exit-on-failure at startup, or "a hardcoded fallback prompt compiled into the client and used when both the local cache is empty and the... API is unavailable" [guaranteed-availability-langfuse].

That is five distinct mechanisms — TTL client cache, background revalidation, Redis server cache, DB fallback, startup prefetch, plus a compiled-in last resort — engineered because "get me the current production prompt" is a request that must never become an outage. The same shape was arrived at independently in mobile and game remote-configuration practice, which is the strongest available evidence that it is convergent design rather than one vendor's habit: "Games should continue to function smoothly even if a Remote Config fetch is delayed, times out, or returns an error"; "Provide a local default for every Remote Config key"; the service "automatically caches the last successful fetch locally on the device"; and explicitly "Avoid the following patterns: Calling FetchConfigsAsync every frame. Retrying immediately on failure" [what-is-the-recommended-way-to-handle-remote-config-fetch-failures-unity-support].

### 8.2 The architectural decision this forces

This is where the thin-layer discipline earns its keep, and where the actual decision lives — not in the abstract "build versus buy" but in **whether the register sits in the hot path**.

**It must not.** The register pushes approved versions *into* each product's existing runtime store — Bedrock's create/version APIs, Vertex's `create`/`create_version`, or a lightweight store for self-hosted runtimes [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation] — and each product continues to read from the store it already reads from, at the reliability that store already provides. The register pulls usage and cost back via telemetry [gen-ai-opentelemetry]. If the register is down, no inference call fails; only promotions and reporting pause. This is the single highest-leverage scope decision in the design, because it converts a five-mechanism reliability requirement into a zero-mechanism one for the majority of consuming products.

The concession: products with no existing prompt-management surface — a self-hosted LangGraph service with prompts as Python string literals — need *something* to be the store. For those, and only those, the register serves prompts directly, and for that path the full cache-plus-TTL-plus-async-refresh-plus-compiled-fallback shape must be implemented, because there is no vendor underneath to inherit it from [caching-langfuse] [guaranteed-availability-langfuse]. This is a real, scoped, acknowledged build cost, and it is medium-confidence whether it is achievable at the documented standard on a short timeline — a requirement, not a demonstrated feasibility.

### 8.3 Parity and the two-sources-of-truth failure

A push architecture creates a specific hazard: the register says v5 is production, and someone with console access edited the Bedrock prompt directly. §2.3's ownership agreement decides who wins; the adapter must **enforce** it with a periodic reconciliation pass that compares each runtime's current pointer and content hash against the register's, and raises drift as an alert with the runtime's value shown rather than silently overwriting it. Silent overwrite is the wrong default because it destroys the evidence that a governance bypass occurred, which is the one thing the register exists to record.

The SDK contract for developers ("Quân", user #3) should be deliberately small: fetch by name and label, receive prompt content plus version identity plus the asset's compatibility-declared metadata, and link every generation to the exact version that produced it so per-version metrics separate cleanly [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. The schema this returns is governed by the BACKWARD compatibility declaration from §4.3 [schema-evolution-compatibility-types-backward-forward-full-transitive-confluent]. Consumers on different SDK versions need a documented guarantee, not an implicit one.

## 9. The Agent Layer and the Build / Buy / Layer Decision

### 9.1 The four surfaces, and the test that produces them

The decision rule is not "build versus buy." It is: **what is structurally unavailable from any vendor at any price?** A disciplined framework asks two questions that matter here — Complexity ("Do existing solutions fit your needs or are your requirements bespoke?") and Core Capability ("Would you be building something that forms part of your company's product or service offering?") [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. Apply them honestly.

On Complexity, version storage, immutable numbering, draft states, diff views and playground/variant-compare UI are emphatically **not** bespoke. Bedrock ships them, Vertex ships them, Langfuse ships them self-hosted [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation] [version-control-langfuse]. By the framework's own test, reuse wins. On Core Capability, cross-account cross-provider aggregation carrying an owner/lifecycle/cost schema, and a real approval-decision object, are sold by nobody — the first because it requires reading from N vendor accounts and normalizing into one schema, the second because every vendor implements promotion as a pointer write [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. There the answer flips to build.

The maintenance economics reinforce the same boundary from the other side. Internal tools fail on day two, not day one: "It's straightforward enough to get a version of whatever you're building working on one person's computer. What next? Where is the app going to live?... Who's going to take responsibility for updating libraries, or maintaining test cases?" [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. The same source records an internal password manager that consumed "somewhere in 4–6 person months" and would have been cheaper to buy, and an internal expenses tool whose feature requests — culminating, tellingly, in "an approvals step, where expenses needed sign-off from a manager" — turned a productivity project into a burden [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. And a production-engineering heuristic sharpens the test into one question: "by reducing a specific tech debt, can I increase developer productivity and deliver business value right now? If I can't, then I don't pay it down" [paying-down-tech-debt-by-gergely-orosz-and-lou-franco]. Read in reverse for new builds: **every built surface is a standing maintenance liability someone must staff.** A competition-origin tool that becomes real conglomerate infrastructure inherits the multi-year maintenance burden without the multi-year team.

That is how the list gets to exactly four: cross-provider asset schema, approval-decision object, per-runtime push adapters, non-content telemetry ingestion. Everything else is inherited.

### 9.2 The agent: what it should and should not do

The brief mandates an agent that "tự đề xuất cải tiến." The literal capability is commoditized — the closest shipped competitor's optimizer already fires from human annotations ("After adding thumbs up/down reactions or free text annotations to playground outputs, select Optimize...") and gates consequential actions behind confirmation [loop-agent-braintrust]. Competing head-on there is a poor use of a small team.

More importantly, ungated generic improvement is measurably dangerous. A controlled ablation found "generic prompt additions do not produce monotonic improvements," with the largest observed decline taking RAG grounding "from 26/30 to 9/30" — 86.7% to 30%, a 56.7-point collapse — because "The improved prompt's instruction to 'be helpful' and 'provide comprehensive answers' encouraged the models to hallucinate information not present in the source documents" [260122025-when-generic-prompt-improvements-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for] [when-better-prompts-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for-llm-applications-a-fram]. The same generic edit *improved* instruction-following by 6.7–13.3 points. There is no scalar "better prompt"; improvement is dimension-specific. The paper calls its own findings "existence proofs... rather than universal quantitative claims" on small synthetic suites with two local 7–8B models — the transferable part is the mechanism, not the percentages. And the rationale an optimizer attaches to its own edit is not a derivation: "While such methods often result in a performance improvement, our experiments suggest that the gradient analogy does not accurately explain their behavior" [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat] — a claim available here only at abstract level, and one that should not be leaned on harder than an abstract supports.

So the agent's scope, in this design, is three jobs:

**(a) Ingestion and mapping** — scan, extract, cluster, and render the state of the world (§3). This is the lead differentiator and no competitor in this record does it.

**(b) Evidence-card assembly** — take a candidate version and produce the frozen evidence record the approval object requires: consequence-language diff, per-category contract results, advisory quality signal with judge model named, cost-per-successful-task delta, blast radius, recommendation. **This is the agent's real output, and its target is the approval object.**

**(c) Rate-capped exception reporting** — the "propose improvements" mandate, narrowed rather than dropped. It fires only for prompts that already have a task-specific eval suite bootstrapped from that prompt's own traffic; only when a failure cluster or cost signal crosses a threshold; and every candidate must clear a held-out slice of that same suite before a human sees it, mirroring the train/validation/test discipline an open-source optimizer implementer describes — "40% train, 40% validation, and 20% test," so "even if a prompt overfits the training examples, it will fail on validation and be discarded" [how-we-built-open-source-automated-prompt-optimization]. Two honesty notes on that source: the split is asserted design intent, and it reports **no quantified train/test generalization gap** — that number does not exist there and must not be invented. Rate discipline should be explicit: at most one active candidate per prompt awaiting decision, never a batch of variants. That cap is a reasoned default transferred from review-overload prescriptions, not an evidence-backed number [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware].

### 9.3 The keystone claim, stated as such

Now the convergence. Three independently-derived positions land on the same component. The build/buy analysis sanctions the approval-decision object as one of exactly four surfaces worth building, because it is genuinely bespoke [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. The vendor survey confirms it as a market gap with zero counter-evidence across four surfaces, verified down to a Postgres column in one case [version-control-langfuse] [release-labels-promptlayer] [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]. And the agent-scope analysis identifies it as the agent's output target, because the evidence card is what keeps the gate from decaying into rubber-stamping [260122025-when-generic-prompt-improvements-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for] [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware].

Three requirements, derived from three different bodies of evidence, satisfied by one component that nobody sells. **Build that first.** Everything else in this report is downstream of it: the schema exists to give the evidence card fields to display; the adapters exist to execute what approval decides; the telemetry exists to supply the cost line on the card.

### 9.4 Confronting the minimalism trap directly

The obvious objection to this angle: the three things that make the product distinctive — the ingestion/clustering agent, the embedded capture surfaces, and the cost-as-gate mechanic — are not all on the four-surface list. A layer thin enough to be maintainable may be too thin to be worth adopting. That objection is correct on its own terms and the answer is not arbitration, it is **sequencing across two different questions**.

The four-surface discipline answers *what gets built durably* — what a small team should still be operating in three years, with reliability guarantees and a maintenance budget. The differentiators answer *what gets built first and thinly* — what wins the first session and the demo. These are compatible because they have different quality bars. The ingestion sweep can ship as a batch job with an embedding-similarity clustering pass and a manual connector per source; it does not need to be a real-time service, it does not sit in anyone's inference path, and if it is down nothing breaks. The capture surface can ship as a forward-to-bot endpoint before it is ever a browser extension. Neither carries the five-mechanism reliability burden of §8.1, which is exactly why they are cheap to build first and dangerous to promise durably.

The precise formulation: **the four surfaces are what you must be able to operate; the differentiators are what you must be able to demonstrate.** And one of them changes category on inspection — duplicate clustering is not a first-session nicety at all. Because label-based versioning has no merge semantics (§4.2), clustering is the only mechanism that catches divergent edits, which makes it a **permanent operational control** and therefore, on this angle's own logic, arguably a fifth durable surface. That is a concession this angle should make rather than resist, and it is the strongest version of the differentiation argument: the differentiator is load-bearing because the architecture is lossy.

## 10. Adoption Inside the Organization

### 10.1 The competitor is friction, not Langfuse

The win condition is "used more than the Google Sheet inside this conglomerate," and adjacent-domain diagnostics converge on one mechanism: "Teams revert to spreadsheets when the official system feels slower than the workaround, harder to trust, or disconnected from how the work actually happens," with the leading root cause stated flatly — "The official platform adds more steps than it removes" [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. Ownership decay produces the same reversion: "If no one governs fields, stages, handoffs, or data quality, the system degrades fast... The spreadsheet then feels safer because the team controls the logic directly" [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets]. And a specific warning against this product's temptation: "buying another platform rarely fixes spreadsheet reversion if process design is weak... Automation exists, but has no clear job... Bad automation creates noise instead of leverage" [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets].

The same mechanism appears from data governance as the entry ticket [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail], and from data observability as manual entry [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery]. Four unrelated domains, one finding: **the labor of populating and operating the official system is what kills it**, not the concept of centralization.

### 10.2 What this angle adds to the adoption argument

The thin-layer discipline has an adoption payoff that is usually missed, and it is worth stating as this section's committed position. A push-not-serve architecture (§8.2) means **product teams do not have to migrate.** The migration rule from operational practice is categorical: "The most common migration failure is asking a team to abandon a working tool before the replacement can do what the tool does" [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog]. A design that says to a Bedrock-committed team "keep Bedrock, we sync into it" makes an adoption ask an order of magnitude smaller than "route your production traffic through our service." For the developer persona, the thin layer is not a compromise on ambition — it is the adoption strategy.

For the non-technical persona, the same logic runs through ingestion: the first session must show *their own* prompts already deduplicated and browsable, never a blank state demanding entry, and the mandatory wall must appear exactly once, at promote-to-production, and nowhere else [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets] [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery]. Everything else — drafting, editing, versioning, running an eval — must be as unwalled as typing in a cell. Governance depth is what sustains retention from week four; it is not what wins session one, and shipping it first reads as automation with no clear job.

### 10.3 The re-fragmentation clock

There is a specific reason not to defer the capture surface indefinitely: "AI tools used individually by team members without integration into the shared production environment are a new category of shadow workflow... When every team member uses their own AI, the shadow data problem scales significantly faster than it did in the spreadsheet era" [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog]. A one-time ingestion sweep converts the historical backlog; it does nothing about prompts written the day after. Without a lightweight embedded capture point at the moment of creation, the register re-accumulates the exact problem it launched to solve. That is the honest reason the capture surface belongs in the sequence early even though it is not one of the four durable surfaces.

Two limits on all of this must be stated. **No source in this record empirically measures non-technical business-user adoption of any AI-artifact register**; the entire adoption argument is analogically transferred from CRM, data-catalog, campaign-tracker and data-observability contexts, none controlled, none N-reported, none prompt-specific [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets] [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail] [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery] [how-hidden-spreadsheets-shadow-workflows-drain-budgets-mtm-blog]. And **nothing in this record addresses Vietnamese-conglomerate organizational behaviour** — whether Zalo rather than Slack is the real incumbent chat channel, or how approval hierarchy actually works there. All six Vietnam-specific sources are legal analyses. The capture-surface design should treat "which chat tool" as a discovery question for the pilot, not a settled assumption.

## 11. Build Sequence and Demo Proof Points

### 11.1 Sequence, ordered by what each step unlocks

**Phase 0 — the schema and the approval object (build durably, first).** The `PromptAsset` and `ApprovalDecision` tables, the state machine, the actor≠reviewer constraint, the frozen evidence record, and per-field provenance stamping [servicenow-cmdb-best-practices-2026-guide-virima]. This is first because it is the keystone (§9.3) and because every later surface writes into it. It is also, usefully, the cheapest of the four in engineering terms — it is a data model and a state machine, not a distributed system.

**Phase 1 — ingestion and clustering (build thinly, first).** Connectors for Sheets/Drive/Notion plus a chat-export parser plus repo scanning [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili], embedding-based clustering [retsim-resilient-and-efficient-text-similarity], `discovered` state, discovery-method provenance per record. Batch, not real-time. This wins session one and the demo.

**Phase 2 — inline authoring and one adapter.** Silent-autosave editing with no submit button; one push adapter end-to-end (whichever runtime the pilot product actually uses) with reconciliation; the SDK fetch-by-label contract with a declared BACKWARD compatibility mode [schema-evolution-compatibility-types-backward-forward-full-transitive-confluent].

**Phase 3 — the contract gate and telemetry.** Deterministic per-category contract checks [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain], telemetry ingestion on non-content attributes, cost-per-successful-task computation with correct cache accounting [gen-ai-opentelemetry] [llm-cost-attribution-at-scale-metadata-tagging-team-budgets-and-chargeback-repor]. This is what makes the evidence card carry real numbers rather than placeholders.

**Phase 4 — the agent's exception-reporting mode, scheduled contract re-runs, RBAC tiering.** Advisory quality signals, drift monitoring, contract-edit permissions separated from prompt-edit permissions.

The ordering principle is explicit: **build the thing nobody sells before building anything anybody sells**, and build the thing that wins the room before building the thing that sustains the quarter.

### 11.2 Three demo beats, one persona, no engineer at the keyboard

1. **The sweep.** A real or seeded Sheet/Notion/chat export ingested live and rendered as a deduplicated, browsable grid within the session — rows are prompts, columns are owner/stage/version/cost. The beat lands because the audience recognizes the mess as theirs, and because the grid mimics the mental model of the tool being displaced rather than inventing a new one.
2. **The edit.** An inline edit with no save button producing a new version automatically, then a one-click rollback by repointing the label — with the honest sixty-second cache-TTL caveat shown rather than hidden [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse].
3. **The gate.** Exactly one visible moment of deliberate friction: the promote click producing a single evidence card — consequence-language diff, per-category contract results, cost delta, blast radius, recommendation — and a decision that writes an approval record. Then show the thing no competitor can show: the same prompt's record afterward, with owner, production pointer, consuming products and cost **all populated by the click and none of them typed by a human.**

Beat three is the whole pitch, because it demonstrates the keystone claim as a mechanism rather than asserting it as a slogan.

### 11.3 What deliberately is not in the demo

No tracing UI, no span waterfall, no generic "AI suggests a better prompt" moment. The first two are commodity observability the product has explicitly declined to build; the third invites a comparison against better-funded optimizers on their strongest axis, while the evidence shows generic improvement can collapse a specific dimension by tens of points [260122025-when-generic-prompt-improvements-hurt-evaluation-driven-iteration-for]. Discipline about what to leave out is the same discipline that produced the four-surface list, applied to twelve minutes instead of twelve months.

## 12. Where the Framing Breaks — Risks and Counter-Evidence

### 12.1 State the asymmetry plainly

**The evidence for this product is weaker than the evidence against it.** The positive case rests on: a founding premise asserted rather than measured for this setting (the duplication study samples a git-structured, majority-public-collection population [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices]); adoption evidence transferred analogically from unrelated domains with no controlled study and no reported N [why-your-team-keeps-reverting-to-spreadsheets] [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]; and a differentiating ingestion capability for which no source shows any competitor doing it — which is an argument for novelty, not efficacy. The negative case rests on MLflow reversing its own lifecycle design after "extensive feedback" [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github], configuration-register field-ownership rot [cmdb-vs-asset-management-differences-and-why-you-need-both], data-catalog adoption failure [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail] [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery], and feature-store scepticism [you-still-dont-need-a-feature-store-xebia] — all disinterested, all from mature categories, none selling this product.

There is one countervailing observation worth recording as a finding: **no source in this record argues against centralizing and governing prompts on the merits.** Every negative voice critiques an implementation — external CAB approval, manual-entry catalogs, over-built feature stores, rigid stage enums — while treating governance of the underlying artifact as sound in principle. The strongest available opposition is about *how*, not *whether*. That is a real, if modest, asymmetry in the other direction, and it is why the correct response to the negative evidence is design discipline rather than abandonment.

### 12.2 The flagship differentiator manufactures the decay it exists to prevent

This is the sharpest internal contradiction in the design and it must be resolved, not smoothed. The two-tier schema (§2.2) works only for a register whose promote-approval action genuinely touches every version that matters. But the flagship move is bulk import that asks nothing. **Those records never pass the one moment where meaning gets captured.** On day 30 the register is large, browsable, and semantically empty — precisely the condition the catalog literature identifies as the onset of trust collapse [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail] [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery] [3-data-catalog-pitfalls-to-avoid-for-successful-governance].

The position taken here, in order of preference:

1. **A distinct `discovered` state, visually and structurally separate from the governed ledger.** Imported records are a *map of the mess*, explicitly labelled as such, filtered out of the governed view by default. This is the honest two-population design: the sweep's value is that it shows you the problem, not that it pretends to have solved it.
2. **First *use* as the capture trigger, not first promotion.** When anyone fetches, edits, or references a `discovered` record, the register asks one question — "what is this for?" — with an AI-drafted answer to confirm or correct. This uses attention that already exists rather than manufacturing a stewardship task.
3. **Explicit expiry.** A `discovered` record untouched for 90 days is auto-proposed for retirement in a batch review. This is what gives the lifecycle a real retirement path (§2.4) instead of a state nothing ever reaches.

What this rejects is "progressive enrichment" as a plan. Enrichment that has no capture moment attached is a wish.

### 12.3 Where the keystone claim could break

Honesty requires naming what would falsify the central argument. Three things would:

**A vendor ships a real approval object.** Convergence across four surfaces with zero counter-evidence is strong but is a snapshot [version-control-langfuse] [release-labels-promptlayer] [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. A Langfuse or Braintrust release implementing a pending-review plus evidence-record plus rejection-path state machine would convert the keystone from a moat into a feature. Mitigation: the *cross-provider* half of the claim would survive, because a single vendor's approval object would govern only its own store.

**Cross-account sharing turns out to cover prompt resources.** Not verified here (§2.1). Even if it did, it would shrink the gap within one cloud without spanning two.

**Promote-time confirmation gets rubber-stamped as reliably as a standalone stewardship task.** If so, the "collapse capture into the promote step" fix does not solve the entry-ticket problem — it relocates it — and the semantic tier should be described with much less confidence. No source in this record resolves this empirically for prompt approval, which is why §6.5 pre-commits to a scope cut rather than a hope [rubber-stamp-reviews-minware].

### 12.4 Do not over-borrow the abstraction

A useful corrective: much of the "asset registry" pattern in circulation is borrowed from feature-store marketing, where "Other content on feature stores is often written by feature store providers," and only a subset of the canonical justifications transfer — "'prevent repeated computation' and 'train-serve skew' do not apply to prompts at all" [you-still-dont-need-a-feature-store-xebia]. Take the ownership-and-lifecycle half; leave the computation-and-serving half. This is the same discipline as the four-surface list, applied to concepts rather than components.

### 12.5 Audited absences carried forward

Five gaps this report carries rather than papers over: no measurement of duplication in a single organization's document estate; no empirical study of non-technical adoption of any AI-artifact register; no study of judge validity on marketing or brand-voice copy; no Vietnam-specific organizational-behaviour evidence (all six Vietnam sources are legal); and the textual-gradients critique available at abstract level only, with both full-text fetches failing [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat]. A sixth, smaller one: the widely-expected train/test generalization gap figure does not exist in the optimizer source it is usually attributed to [how-we-built-open-source-automated-prompt-optimization]. Each is a finding about the state of the evidence, and each bounds a claim above.

## Source Tensions

### T1. Fewer tokens versus lower cost

Both sides are right about different measures and the disagreement dissolves into a design rule. Compression research measures input tokens under compression engineered to preserve task-relevant information, reporting up to 20x at under 2% quality loss [prompt-compression-cut-token-costs-without-losing-quality-neuraltrust], with a formal frontier establishing that meaningful compression is achievable in principle [240715504-fundamental-limits-of-prompt-compression-a-rate-distortion-framework-f]. The counter-case measures end-to-end session cost when compression destroys information the task needed: a 17% input cut producing a 67% total cost increase [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse]. **Resolution: prompt length is not cost.** The claim to make is not "compression does not work" but the narrower, better-supported "input-token delta is not a valid proxy for cost delta" — which is all the design decision requires. Build the gate on cost per successful completed task over a traffic sample including retries, attributed to the prompt version (§7.2).

### T2. Judges that match humans versus judges that cannot rank at all

Side B wins, and it wins by *explaining* side A rather than contradicting it. The variable is criterion-referenceability. Where a task has a checkable target, judges reach near-human agreement — ρ>0.6 blind on structured questions, 0.885 with solutions, >0.84 on rubric-constrained plots [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass]; where the target is holistic and human raters themselves reach ρ=0.054, the judge carries no rank information at all (ρ≈0, all CIs spanning zero). The frequently-cited ">80% agreement" figure comes from exactly the verifiable end of that axis under a 2023 protocol [230605685-judging-llm-as-a-judge-with-mt-bench-and-chatbot-arena], and a more adversarial-aware study caps best-effort debiased agreement at 71.0% [judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm]. This is decisive rather than interesting because **the gate is most trustworthy on the work this product handles least and least trustworthy on the work it handles most.** The consequence is the split of §5.2, and the analogy limit (essay marking as proxy for copy) is labelled every time it is used. Practitioner experience corroborates from a different direction: interpretive, ad-hoc evaluation practices "are necessary adaptations to LLM characteristics rather than methodological failures" [260416304-results-actionability-gap-understanding-how-practitioners-evaluate-llm].

### T3. The register as destination versus discovery in the flow

The synthesis is a split by function, and the word "catalogue" is what conflates them. A data catalog indexes assets living elsewhere; a prompt register *is* the asset, and the serving path requires a canonical store [prompt-governance-is-the-new-data-governance-cio] [caching-langfuse]. So the ledger must be a destination. But the moment a prompt is *written* today happens in a chat thread or a Sheet cell, and requiring a special trip to file it reproduces the manual-entry failure that kills catalogs [data-catalogs-are-dead-long-live-data-discovery] [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. **Destination for the ledger, embedded for capture.** The testable consequence: if the only way to add a prompt is to visit the register, the register holds the prompts people remembered to file — precisely the population an asset ledger is useless for. This angle adds one refinement: the capture surface is a *thin, first* build, not a durable one — a forward-to-bot endpoint before a browser extension.

### T4. Approval as a drag on delivery versus approval as accountability

Largely dissolved by noticing what was measured: approval by a body **external** to the change, lacking context, reconstructing the reasoning itself [dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval] [when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz]. The prescribed remedy is peer review embedded in the platform by someone close to the change — structurally what an accountable owner deciding from a pre-computed evidence card already is. Two honesty requirements follow. The scale caveat is real: the theatre mechanism is throughput-driven and would need revisiting at hundreds of promotions per week [the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]. And the legal basis must be stated correctly — neither the EU record-keeping mandate [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act] nor Vietnam's Law and Decree [companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new] [update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi] obliges a pre-release human sign-off on an internal prompt-management layer. **Segregation of duties plus prophylactic readiness, not statute.**

### T5. What a prompt inventory can actually see

Neither security-industry method sees the population this product exists to govern, and that is the finding rather than a gap in the analysis. Egress capture observes traffic but cannot attribute it to an authored, versioned prompt, and sees nothing about a prompt sitting unused in a Notion page [shadow-ai-discovery-a-critical-part-of-enterprise-ai-governance]. Source scanning sees code and is blind to Sheets, Drive, chat threads and shared accounts [static-analysis-for-llm-prompt-security-a-methodology-for-pre-deploy-vulnerabili]. The two are complementary and jointly incomplete, and the space between them is exactly the document-and-chat estate. **Document-source connectors are a first-class third method**, and the register must record which method found each record so coverage claims stay honest. The shadow-AI material is derivative and is used for method only, never for prevalence figures.

### T6. Whether a prompt is an asset in any sense that survives scrutiny

Side B is right that "asset" fails a formal accounting test — identifiability, control, probable benefits, reliable cost measurement, with know-how-type internally generated items expensed because their cost "cannot be distinguished from expenditure to develop the business as a whole" [recognition-and-cost-of-intangible-assets-ias-38-ifrs-community] — and right that the pro-asset chorus is commercially interested. Side A survives anyway because the claim doing the work is **managerial**. "Sổ tài sản" earns its keep if and only if the register carries a named owner, a lifecycle with a retirement path, a record of what uses it, and a cost — and it should be judged on whether those fields are populated and true, not on whether a prompt could reach a balance sheet. The cost of getting this wrong is specific: an unearned asset claim invites the reasonable objection that this is a database with an expensive name. The honest version — a ledger whose fields are derived from actions people already take — is both more defensible and, usefully, a stricter design constraint.

### T7. Git's branching versus the label pointer a non-developer can move

Side B follows necessarily from the fixed choice of the non-developer as primary user, so the architecture is settled: rollback is repointing a label, with the application picking it up on next fetch [version-control-langfuse] [release-labels-promptlayer], and git-based approaches are ill-suited where content changes rapidly [api-first-cms-vs-git-based]. But side A names a cost that must be paid explicitly rather than waved away [git-based-cms-vs-api-driven-cms-which-headless-cms-should-you-choose-bejamas]. Label-based versioning gives linear history with movable pointers, **not divergent branches with reconciliation.** Two people editing in different directions produce colliding linear versions or a near-duplicate under a different name, and nothing in the versioning primitive catches it. Only clustering catches it, after the fact. **That makes duplicate clustering the standing compensating control for an architecture that cannot merge** — which is why §9.4 concedes it may deserve durable-surface status, and why it must run continuously against the live register rather than once at migration.

## Opinionated Synthesis

The register that should be built is small, and its smallness is the argument rather than an apology for it. Four surfaces are genuinely bespoke — the cross-provider asset schema, the approval-decision object, the per-runtime push adapters, and non-content telemetry ingestion — because each answers a requirement that no vendor sells at any price. The scoping test that produces that list is not "build versus buy"; it is "what is structurally unavailable?" Bedrock's prompts live in one account, Vertex's live in one project, and no amount of spend produces a Vertex-side view of a Bedrock prompt [create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock] [prompt-management-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform-google-cloud-documentation]. Everything else the brief asks for is already built, free, and operated behind a five-mechanism reliability pattern — TTL cache, background revalidation, Redis, DB fallback, startup prefetch, compiled-in last resort — that exists because serving a production prompt must never become an outage [caching-langfuse] [guaranteed-availability-langfuse]. Rebuilding that would be the most expensive way to arrive back where you started.

The approval-decision object is the keystone, and the case for it is the strongest argument in this report because it is over-determined. It is the confirmed gap — four vendor surfaces, zero counter-evidence, verified down to a Postgres column and conceded in a vendor's own capability table, where "Multi-step approval workflow" sits in the Composed column [update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github] [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. It is the sanctioned bespoke surface under an honest complexity-and-core-capability test [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. And it is where the agent's real work lands, because what a human should review is an assembled evidence card, not a raw diff with a machine's unreliable account of its own reasoning [251213598-textual-gradients-are-a-flawed-metaphor-for-automatic-prompt-optimizat]. Build it first. It is also, conveniently, the cheapest of the four — a data model and a state machine.

Three commitments keep the design honest. The eval gate blocks on deterministic contract properties, reports subjective quality as advisory, and re-runs its contract on a schedule against the live model — because judges collapse to no rank information on holistic copy [criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass] and because drift with no version change is documented at 84%→51% and 52%→10% [prompt-drift-what-it-is-and-how-to-detect-it-agenta-blog] [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. The cost gate measures cost per successful completed task, never prompt length, because a 17% token cut has been observed raising session cost 67% [why-shorter-prompts-can-make-coding-agents-worse]. And mandatory HITL rests on segregation of duties and prophylactic readiness, not on a statute that does not currently bind the register [article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act] [companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new].

The minimalism trap is real and the answer is sequencing with two different quality bars. The ingestion sweep, the clustering, and the capture surface get built first and thinly — batch jobs and endpoints, nothing in anyone's inference path, nothing promising uptime. The four surfaces get built durably, because they are what someone will still be operating in three years, and because every built surface is a standing maintenance liability [paying-down-tech-debt-by-gergely-orosz-and-lou-franco] [build-vs-buy-a-guide-for-internal-tools-retool-blog]. The one concession this angle owes: clustering is not merely a first-session flourish. A label-pointer architecture cannot merge divergent edits, so clustering is the only mechanism that catches them — which makes it a permanent control and, on this angle's own logic, a candidate fifth durable surface.

Looking forward, three forces will decide whether this design ages well. First, the vendor gap will close on the single-provider half: it is a reasonable expectation that some vendor ships a pending-review state within a release cycle or two, since composing one from protected labels, CI evidence and webhooks is already documented as the workaround [prompt-cicd-version-gate-and-roll-out-prompts-like-code-langfuse]. The cross-provider half will not close, because it requires a vendor to build a first-class view of a competitor's resources. The durable moat is therefore aggregation, and the approval object is what makes aggregation worth having rather than the moat itself — a distinction worth being clear-eyed about now rather than after a competitor ships. Second, if providers begin publishing deployer-verifiable behavioural compatibility commitments, drift becomes partially pre-release-anticipatable and the monitoring pillar shrinks; until then it is load-bearing, because "the provider does not define a behavioral specification that deployer can test against" [test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain]. Third, and most consequentially for a Vietnamese group: as the group's own products classify into medium- and high-risk tiers under the Law's risk regime, with a 60-day notification clock already run and 12- or 18-month compliance clocks running to March and September 2027 [update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi] [vietnam-artificial-intelligence-law-foundation-and-outlook-insight-baker-mckenzi] [vietnam-the-first-law-on-artificial-intelligence-what-you-must-know-duane-morris], the approval record stops being an internal control and becomes a compliance artifact those product teams need and cannot easily reconstruct. That is the register's strongest medium-term claim on budget, and it is available only to a design that recorded the decision rather than merely gating the write.

The final judgement is a wager with a stated basis. The evidence for this product is thinner than the evidence against it, and the strongest opposition is disinterested while the strongest support is vendor-authored [understanding-prompt-management-in-github-repositories-a-call-for-best-practices] [rfc-deprecating-model-registry-stages-issue-10336-mlflowmlflow-github] [why-do-data-catalogue-projects-fail]. But every disinterested critic in this record attacks an implementation — manual entry, external approval, rigid stage enums, over-built abstraction — and none attacks the premise that a group should know which prompts it owns, who owns them, what runs in production, and what they cost. That is a governance problem whose answer is design discipline, and design discipline here has a precise meaning: derive every operational field from an action someone was already taking, build only what cannot be bought, and put the one thing nobody sells at the centre.
