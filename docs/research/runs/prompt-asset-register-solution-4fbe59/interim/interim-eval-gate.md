# Interim report: what-the-eval-gate-can-honestly-promise

**Locus question:** Given task-dependent judge validity, irreducible self-preference bias, and provider-side drift no pre-release test can see, what can a regression gate actually guarantee — and who must own the eval criteria for it to mean anything?
**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep had already assembled the raw materials for this locus without resolving the tension: papers establishing LLM-as-judge agreement statistics (MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena, self-preference bias, bias-mitigation), a fresh arXiv paper specifically on physics-marking validity by task type, a supply-chain governance paper on drift, three practitioner blog posts distinguishing regression from drift, and two "evals are the real bottleneck" essays (Hamel, Applied LLMs). No corpus note yet reconciled these into a single design rule for what a "regression eval to guarantee no quality degradation" can honestly deliver, or who should be allowed to define the pass/fail line — which is exactly the gap this investigation closes.

## What the new sources say

**Physics judging paper — full text, `criterion-referenceability-determines-llm-as-a-judge-validity-across-physics-ass`** (arXiv:2603.14732v1, Yeadon et al., Durham University). This is the single most decisive source in the corpus for this locus. It tests five frontier judges (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini Pro 3, DeepSeek-V3.2, Grok 4.1) across three physics assessment formats and reports both absolute error (MAE) and *discriminative validity* (Spearman ρ — the ability to correctly rank-order responses by quality) separately, which is the methodological move that makes the paper load-bearing: "a system can achieve low MAE and good score-scale alignment while entirely failing to distinguish between good and poor student work."

- **Structured questions** (n=771 blind exam questions): "models achieve fractional mean absolute errors (fMAE) ≈0.22 with robust discriminative validity (Spearman ρ>0.6)." With official solutions supplied, committee ρ=0.885 (Claude Opus 4.5, solution condition).
- **Essays** (n=55 scripts / 275 essays, holistic 0–100 marking): "blind AI marking is harsher and more variable than human marking, with discriminative validity already poor (ρ≈0.1). Adding a mark scheme does not improve discrimination (ρ≈0; all confidence intervals include zero)." The committee's blind ρ is 0.088, scheme ρ is −0.044, scheme+anchor ρ is 0.034 — **every single one of these 95% CIs includes zero.** Anchored exemplars compress the AI score distribution "below even the human standard deviation" and pull the mean within 2.3 marks of the human mean, but "distributional agreement can occur without valid discrimination" — the paper's central warning against reading low MAE as evidence of validity.
- The human baseline itself is the load-bearing fact: "The average human–human MAE was 6.84 marks... rank-order agreement between human markers was very limited, with an average pairwise Spearman correlation of just ρ=0.054... This demonstrates that open-ended physics essay marking is an inherently noisy, highly subjective task where even expert human markers fail to reach consensus on rank ordering."
- **Scientific plots** (n=1400, rubric-constrained but visual/holistic-adjacent): ρ>0.84 across all models — "an order of magnitude higher than those observed in essay marking under any condition."
- The paper's own synthesis construct, **criterion-referenceability** ("the extent to which the features that justify a mark can be made explicit, inspected, and applied consistently"), is explicitly offered as the deployment diagnostic: "Before asking whether LLM-as-a-judge can be used to mark a task, one should first establish whether the task is sufficiently criterion-referenceable that human markers themselves assign stable and discriminative judgements. If human raters cannot achieve acceptable rank agreement, an LLM is unlikely to recover valid ordering merely by being given more examples or guidance."
- Governance framing directly on point: "The key governance question is therefore not 'does the AI look roughly right on average?', but 'does this task admit a sufficiently valid and reliable marking process for AI outputs to be trusted in the first place?'"

**Judging-the-judges — full text, `judging-the-judges-a-systematic-evaluation-of-bias-mitigation-strategies-in-llm`** (arXiv:2604.23178v2, Soumik). Tests nine debiasing strategies across five judges on MT-Bench (n=400), LLMBar (n=200, adversarial), and a custom 375-pair bias-isolation dataset. Establishes the *ceiling* on how good a debiased judge gets even on natural preference data, and shows debiasing is not free — it can help or hurt depending on task adversarialness:
- Best achievable configuration: "Gemini 2.5 Flash with the Combined Budget strategy achieves the highest agreement of any configuration we tested (71.0%, κ=0.549, p<0.0001) at ∼$0.001 per evaluation." Even the best frontier configuration, Claude Sonnet 4 + same strategy, reaches only 69.5%.
- Position swap — the single most common LLM-judge debiasing technique — is *harmful* on adversarial/high-stakes items: "On LLMBar's adversarial instances, position swap consistently and significantly hurts all models (−3 to −13 pp; three Holm-Bonferroni-significant negatives), as tie resolution discards correct verdicts on clear-cut cases." This is a direct warning against a naive "debias and gate" design — the correct mitigation is task-dependent, not a fixed pipeline.
- Style bias, not position bias, is the dominant residual bias: "Four of five models show strong style bias (Pro +0.76, Flash +0.72, Claude +0.68, Llama +0.40)... overwhelmingly preferring markdown-formatted responses over plain prose when content is identical." A human-annotation check found humans prefer markdown only 57% of the time versus 73–97% for four of five judges — i.e. the judges' stylistic preference is itself miscalibrated relative to the human standard they're meant to approximate.

**MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena — abstract only, `230605685`** (Zheng et al. 2023, NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks). The often-cited high-water mark: "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans." Reading this against judging-the-judges' 71% ceiling on a comparable benchmark two years later (with five debiasing strategies applied) is itself informative: the >80% figure was reported for GPT-4-as-judge on curated MT-Bench data under the original paper's specific protocol and is not a number the corpus's more recent adversarial/bias-controlled work reproduces as a general floor.

**Self-preference bias — abstract only, `241021819`** (Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri 2024/2025, NeurIPS Safe GenAI Workshop). Mechanistic account of *why* self-preference bias is not simply prompt-fixable: "LLMs assign significantly higher evaluations to outputs with lower perplexity than human evaluators, regardless of whether the outputs were self-generated... the self-preference bias exists because LLMs prefer texts more familiar to them." This matters architecturally: a prompt-registry agent that both *suggests* prompt improvements and *judges* the resulting outputs is at elevated structural risk of self-preference bias if judge and improver share a model family, because the bias tracks perplexity/familiarity, not a labeled "is this my own output" signal that could be prompted away.

**Results-Actionability Gap — abstract only, `260416304`** (van der Maden et al. 2026, cs.SE). Nineteen practitioner interviews found "practitioners gather evaluation data but cannot translate findings into concrete improvements" (the "results-actionability gap") and that "interpretive practices are necessary adaptations to LLM characteristics rather than methodological failures" — i.e. ad-hoc vibe checks are not a maturity failure to be automated away but a rational response to the underlying validity problem this locus documents.

**Test Before You Deploy — full text, `test-before-you-deploy-governing-updates-in-the-llm-supply-chain`** (arXiv:2604.27789v1, Chishti, Oyinloye, Li, NTNU, 2026). This is the paper that operationalizes the block/report split and confirms drift is structurally distinct from regression. Three-part framework, captured verbatim for implementability:
- **Production contracts**: "explicit behavioral requirements that must hold for safe deployment. The contracts specify measurable thresholds, such as minimum correctness rates, formatting constraints, and security requirements."
- **Risk-Category-Based Regression Suite**: "organizes evaluation prompts into risk-aligned groups that represent distinct deployment concerns. Each risk category is evaluated independently to detect domain-specific drift that aggregate metrics can mask... logs LLM snapshots, each with a model identifier and execution timestamp, to enable reproducibility and drift attribution." Worked example: Category 1 (backend SQL) needs "a pass rate of more than 95%," Category 2 (JSON output) "requires 100% format compliance," Category 3 (security) "requires 0% security violations" — i.e. thresholds are per-risk-category, not one global score.
- **Compatibility gates**: "compares category-level metrics against production contract thresholds and triggers review workflows when any violation occurs. It shifts the adoption decision from provider-controlled releases to deployer-controlled compatibility checks."
- The paper's own governing distinction, quoted precisely because it is the crux of this locus: silent model updates are "not new to software engineering, and CI/CD quality gates have long been used to manage them. However, such services typically expose versioning and operate under service level agreements. The hosted LLM services are fundamentally different... the provider does not define a behavioral specification that deployer can test against."
- Documented empirical drift, cited inside the paper: "Chen et al. (2024) provide empirical evidence of drift by documenting that direct execution of code generated by GPT-4 drops from 52% to 10% within three months (March-June, 2023), without any version change." Separately: "Anthropic's August 2025 postmortem report documents three bugs affecting Claude Sonnet 4's performance without any API modifications, including routing errors affecting up to 16% of requests and output corruption causing non-ASCII character injection."
- The paper's own exploratory validation (25 prompts × 7 Claude model tiers) found category-differentiated drift exactly as the framework predicts: "The SQL and authentication functions remained stable, while structured JSON tasks showed higher drift... a backend SQL function passed all tests with Sonnet 4, but failed a test for safe encoding next day, suggesting silent infrastructure changes." This is the single cleanest piece of evidence that even a passing gate today gives zero information about tomorrow.
- Open challenges the authors flag as unsolved (i.e., do not oversell a mitigation the corpus doesn't support): calibrating thresholds "without principled statistical calibration," handling per-prompt run-to-run variability ("Some responses passed all checks in one run and failed in another"), and drift attribution ("hosted LLM providers rarely expose version identifiers... deployer-side governance operates under partial observability").

**Prompt Drift (Agenta) — full text, `prompt-drift-what-it-is-and-how-to-detect-it-agenta-blog`**. Supplies the exact figure Analyst A's claim points to, correctly attributed: "The most well-documented case comes from a Stanford and UC Berkeley study published in 2023 (Chen, Zaharia, and Zou, 'How Is ChatGPT's Behavior Changing over Time?'). The researchers tested GPT-4 on identical tasks in March 2023 and June 2023... GPT-4's accuracy on identifying prime numbers dropped from 84% to 51% between the March and June versions." **This is the same underlying paper** (Chen, Zaharia, Zou 2024, *Harvard Data Science Review*) that Test-Before-You-Deploy cites for the 52%→10% code-execution figure — the paper reports multiple task-specific drift magnitudes, not one number; both 84%→51% (prime-number identification) and 52%→10% (code execution success) are real, from the same study, on different tasks. Analyst A's figure is correct and should be attributed to Chen et al. 2024 via this study, not treated as an isolated anecdote. Agenta's own definitional line is the cleanest formal statement of the block/report boundary: "This is different from prompt regression, where a deliberate edit to a prompt causes worse performance. With prompt drift, the prompt stays the same. The world around it shifts... You don't own the model. You can't diff the weights. You can't review the changelog (because there often isn't one)."

**PromptFoo blog — full text, `promptfoo-passes-production-still-breaks-heres-the-gap-dev-community`**. First-person practitioner account of exactly the failure mode this locus is testing: "I had PromptFoo set up in CI. Evals passed on every deployment. The model still silently changed in production three weeks later — without any CI run, without any code change." Documents a concrete failure sequence (Day 0 ship, Day 21 silent OpenAI update, Day 21–24 JSON extraction breaks intermittently ~15% of calls, Day 24 first user complaint, Day 26 root-caused) and states the general principle: "PromptFoo catches regressions at step 1. It doesn't catch steps 2-6... Use both — they cover different failures... Neither does the other's job." This independently corroborates Test-Before-You-Deploy's structural claim using a different toolchain and a different set of models (GPT-5.2, GPT-4o-2024-08-06 pinned, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro all cited as having silently changed in production without triggering CI).

**Towards Data Science — full text, `prompt-engineering-fails-quietly-prompt-regression-is-why-towards-data-science`**. The most directly implementable articulation of the block-worthy design in the whole corpus, and the source of the strongest one-line justification for deterministic gating: "I completely skipped the LLM-as-a-judge route... I realized something important here: regression testing isn't really a quality problem — it's a contract problem. Checking if the output intent matches the expected intent is binary, so a judge model just adds noise." The author's "False Improvement Detection" pattern is the concrete mechanism the product needs: a prompt version can raise the aggregate accuracy score while collapsing a specific critical category (in their benchmark, v4 improved overall accuracy by 10% while negation-handling collapsed 66.7%, from 100.0%→33.3%), and only category-level, deterministic, pre-declared "critical category" tracking catches this — a single scalar eval-delta number, of the kind the product's positioning doc describes ("eval delta" on the evidence card), would not have caught it. Explicit division of labor: "Regression testing, however, demands absolute determinism... The two methods complement each other. Run this regression suite before every prompt commit to intercept structural breaks, and run your LLM-as-a-judge evaluations periodically to audit the open-ended nuances that code-based checks cannot catch."

**Hamel's Blog — full text, `your-ai-product-needs-evals-hamels-blog`**. The canonical source for the "eval loop, not prompt wording, is the bottleneck" claim, and gives the concrete mechanics of judge alignment as *ongoing work requiring an owner*, not a one-time setup: "You should track the correlation between model-based and human evaluation to decide how much you can rely on automatic evaluation... by collecting critiques from labelers explaining why they are making a decision, you can iterate on the evaluator model to align it with humans through prompt engineering." Explicitly frames judge alignment as its own meta-system: "Model-based evaluation is a meta-problem within your larger problem. You must maintain a mini-evaluation system to track its quality." Also directly warns against over-trusting LLM-as-judge even after alignment work: "LLM-as-Judge is not a silver bullet though. There are subtle aspects of language where even the strongest models fail to evaluate reliably... conventional classifiers and reward models can achieve higher accuracy than LLM-as-Judge, and with lower cost and latency."

**Applied LLMs — partial (evals + operational sections), `what-weve-learned-from-a-year-of-building-with-llms-applied-llms`**. Independently corroborates Hamel with a different practitioner cohort and adds two facts directly relevant to who-owns-what: (1) judge-human alignment is achievable but requires iteration and is measured, not assumed — "log the LLM response, judge's critique (i.e., CoT), and final outcome. They are then reviewed with stakeholders to identify areas for improvement. Over three iterations, agreement with humans and LLM improved from 68% to 94%!" — i.e. a raw, unaligned LLM judge starts well below the reliability bar and only gets there through a deliberate, resourced calibration process someone must own; (2) "criteria drift" is named explicitly as a first-class failure mode distinct from model drift: "developers' perceptions of what constitutes 'good' and 'bad' outputs shift as they interact with more data (i.e., criteria drift)... these predefined criteria are often incomplete." This means the eval criteria themselves are not a stable artifact to be set once by whoever happens to hold edit rights — they require continuous, accountable revision, which is precisely the argument for treating "who may change eval criteria" as the real permission boundary rather than "who may edit prompt text."

## Evidence synthesis

### Position A: automated LLM-as-judge evaluation can be trusted to gate promotion — under the right conditions

The strongest version of this position does not claim LLM judges are reliable in general; it claims they are reliable *on criterion-referenceable tasks*. The physics paper's own strongest results support this precisely: structured questions under blind marking already reach ρ=0.696 (committee) and rise to ρ=0.885 with an official solution; scientific plots — a task with observable, rubric-mappable features (axes, labels, scale, correctness) even though it is not a single-correct-answer task — reach ρ>0.84, "an order of magnitude higher than... essay marking." MT-Bench's original >80%-agreement headline, and judging-the-judges' demonstration that targeted debiasing (S8 "Combined Budget": CoT + rubric + position swap) produces statistically significant, Holm-Bonferroni-surviving gains (+11.5pp for Claude, +7.5pp for Gemini Flash, p<0.0001 both) show that judge reliability is not fixed — it responds to engineering effort, and a well-resourced team can move a judge meaningfully closer to human agreement. On this reading, the right design is not "never gate on LLM judgment" but "gate on LLM judgment only for the subset of prompts whose outputs are criterion-referenceable, and invest continuously in judge-human alignment (Hamel's 68%→94% trajectory) for the rest."

### Position B: automated evaluation cannot honestly promise no quality degradation for this product's actual workload

The countervailing evidence is not a competing interpretation of the same data — it is the same paper's other half, and it maps almost exactly onto the product's stated primary user. The physics paper's essay-marking condition is architecturally the closest available analogue to marketing copy and design-brief evaluation: open-ended, holistically judged, no single correct answer, evaluated on tone/coherence/persuasiveness rather than a checkable target. On that task, discriminative validity is "already poor" blind (ρ≈0.1) and stays statistically indistinguishable from zero after every intervention this product's roadmap would reach for — a written mark scheme (ρ≈0, "all confidence intervals include zero") and anchored few-shot exemplars (committee ρ=0.034, 95% CI [−0.22, 0.28]). The paper is explicit that anchoring's apparent improvement is a trap: it compresses variance and shifts the mean toward the human distribution "without any accompanying improvement in discriminative validity" — i.e., a gate built on this pattern would look calibrated on a dashboard while being blind to which version is actually better. Layered on top, even the *best possible* debiased judge on natural preference data (judging-the-judges) tops out at 71.0% agreement (κ=0.549) — nowhere near a threshold anyone would accept as "guaranteeing" no degradation, and that ceiling is on MT-Bench-style general chat preferences, not on holistic marketing/design judgment, where the physics paper's essay result is the closer proxy and shows near-zero, not 71%. Self-preference bias compounds this in a way specific to this product's architecture: because the bias tracks output perplexity/familiarity rather than an identifiable self-signal, an agent that both proposes prompt edits and evaluates their outputs is structurally exposed regardless of prompting discipline, unless judge and generator are deliberately decoupled. And even where a judge is valid today, Test-Before-You-Deploy and its two independent practitioner corroborations (PromptFoo blog, Agenta blog) establish that provider-side drift is not a smaller version of the same problem — it is categorically un-catchable by any pre-release gate, by construction, because the thing that changed is outside the deployer's release cycle entirely ("No pull request. No commit. No CI trigger.").

### Where the tension actually resolves

These are not two readings of ambiguous evidence; they are two different task regimes documented inside the *same* controlled study, and the physics paper supplies the exact instrument for telling them apart before deployment: check whether human raters themselves achieve acceptable rank agreement on the task. Structured questions and plots pass that check (human-plausible ρ, criterion-referenceable); essays fail it outright (human-human ρ=0.054, "even expert human markers fail to reach consensus on rank ordering"). Marketing copy and design briefs are, by construction, closer to the essay condition than the structured-question condition — there is no official solution, no single correct derivation, and disagreement among human marketers/creative directors about what counts as "good" copy is the norm rather than a data-collection failure. Position A's caveat ("under the right conditions") is therefore not a hedge that splits the difference — it is the disqualifying condition for this product's primary use case. The drift evidence (Position B, strand b) is not in tension with Position A at all; it is orthogonal and applies regardless of which position is right on judge validity, because it is about *when* the gate can even run, not how well it scores.

## Committed position

A regression gate that emits a single "no quality degradation" verdict cannot be honestly implemented for this product, and building one would be a fabricated guarantee dressed as governance. The evidence forces a rule with three enforced parts, not one gate: **(1) BLOCK automatically only on deterministic, code-checkable production-contract properties** — schema conformance, required disclaimers, forbidden-claims/banned-phrase lists, length bounds, token/cost delta, latency — computed the way the Towards Data Science piece and Hamel's Level-1 "assertions" describe (binary, reproducible, no LLM in the loop), because this is a contract problem, not a quality problem, and only contract checks can be run at the reliability CI/regression-gate language implies. **(2) REPORT, never auto-verdict, on subjective quality** — LLM-judge/eval-delta scores appear as one signal on the human approver's evidence card, exactly as the product's own "two-minute evidence card" USP already implies, but the corpus shows this signal caps out around 60–71% agreement even under best-effort debiasing on general tasks and is statistically indistinguishable from a coin flip's worth of rank information (ρ≈0, CI spans zero) on the holistic-copy task class this product's users actually produce — so the mandatory HITL clause is not ceremonial, it is the only mechanism in the pipeline capable of exercising the judgment the eval literature shows automation cannot supply for this workload. **(3) MONITOR continuously post-promotion for drift**, because a pre-release-only gate — however good — structurally cannot see the failure mode that two independent named incidents in the corpus (Anthropic's August 2025 postmortem: "routing errors affecting up to 16% of requests"; Chen et al. 2024's GPT-4 accuracy collapses of 84%→51% and 52%→10% with no version change) show is real and recurring; "guarantee no quality degradation" is a claim about a continuously-held property, and the query's own governance framing implicitly demands ongoing re-verification against the same production contract used at promotion time, not a one-time pre-release check. This is a refinement of, not a departure from, the orchestrator's working hypothesis: block/report is correct, but "block/report" alone under-delivers on "guarantee" unless it is paired with scheduled re-runs of the same deterministic contract against the currently-serving model, turning the compatibility gate into a recurring job rather than a promotion-time-only checkpoint. On the RBAC question: the corpus's "eval loop is the bottleneck, not prompt wording" claim (Hamel, Applied LLMs, TDS) is correct, and it means the product's permission model is aimed at the wrong artifact if it stops at "who may edit prompt text." The coherent design keeps prompt-text editing open to marketers/designers (that authorship is genuinely low-stakes and reversible via versioning) while placing edit rights over the production contract — the deterministic thresholds and the critical-category list — under a stricter, reviewed role (the approver "Toàn" or a platform/governance function), because Applied LLMs' "criteria drift" finding shows those thresholds are exactly as unstable and exactly as consequential as the prompts they gate, and leaving them editable by whoever also edits prompts collapses the two-key structure HITL is supposed to provide.

- **Position:** The gate can honestly promise zero degradation only on deterministic, code-checkable contract properties (schema, banned content, cost/latency/length); it must present LLM-judge quality scores as an advisory signal to the human approver, never an automated pass/fail, for the subjective marketing/design copy this product's primary users produce; and it must re-run its deterministic contract on a schedule against the live model, not only at promotion time, or the "guarantee" language is false even for the properties it can check.
- **Confidence:** high on the block/report split and on "cannot honestly guarantee via judge score alone" (converging, independent evidence: a controlled multi-model study with CIs spanning zero, an independent bias-mitigation study capping agreement at 71%, and three independent practitioner accounts of drift bypassing CI). Medium on the specific RBAC redesign (contract-editing vs. prompt-editing as separate permission tiers) — this is a defensible extrapolation from Hamel/Applied LLMs/TDS's bottleneck claim and the physics paper's criterion-referenceability framework, but no source in the corpus studies prompt-registry RBAC design directly, so it is inference from adjacent evidence, not a directly measured result.
- **Boundary conditions:** This position holds specifically for holistic, low-criterion-referenceability outputs (marketing copy, design briefs, brand-voice tone) where no official solution or single correct target exists and human raters themselves show weak inter-rater agreement — the essay-marking analogue. It does NOT hold with the same force for criterion-referenceable prompt outputs inside the same registry (e.g., a prompt producing structured data extraction, a classification label, or a templated document with checkable fields) — there, per the physics paper's structured-question and plot results (ρ up to 0.88, ρ>0.84), a well-specified LLM judge can plausibly support a soft-block gate, and the product should let the human define per-prompt-family which regime applies rather than applying one policy universally.
- **What would change this position:** A rubric-based LLM judge study on marketing/design-copy tasks specifically (not physics essays, not general chat preference) showing (a) human inter-rater ρ meaningfully above the essay paper's 0.054 baseline — i.e., that human copy reviewers themselves agree enough to define a valid target — and (b) an LLM judge achieving ρ>0.6 with a CI excluding zero against that human baseline, would justify allowing at least a soft-block gate for copy tasks specifically. Separately, if LLM providers began shipping deployer-verifiable behavioral-compatibility commitments (the corpus notes this is an open research direction — Liu et al. 2025's "semantic versioning using behavioral fingerprints," cited but not yet operational per Test-Before-You-Deploy) drift would become at least partially pre-release-anticipatable, weakening the case for mandatory continuous post-promotion monitoring as a distinct third pillar.
- **Evidence weight:** 1 controlled multi-format study with n=771/1151/275/1400 across three task types and explicit CIs (physics paper) — primary; 1 independent bias-mitigation study with n=400/200/375 across five models and nine strategies (judging-the-judges) — corroborating on judge ceiling; 1 mechanistic study on self-preference bias origin (perplexity/familiarity) — abstract-level, supporting but thinner; 1 practitioner interview study (n=19) on the "results-actionability gap" — supporting, qualitative; 1 supply-chain governance paper with exploratory validation (25 prompts × 7 model tiers) plus two independently-cited empirical drift incidents (Chen et al. 2024, Anthropic postmortem) — primary for drift; 2 independent practitioner blog accounts of drift bypassing CI in different toolchains (PromptFoo, Agenta) — corroborating; 2 independent practitioner essays on the eval-loop-as-bottleneck claim from different companies/cohorts (Hamel/Rechat, Applied LLMs) — corroborating for the RBAC argument, but neither is a controlled study of governance/permissions specifically. No source in the corpus directly contradicts the core claim that essay-analogue holistic judging is near-zero-validity; the only genuine counter-evidence is the MT-Bench >80% figure, which the more recent, more adversarial-aware judging-the-judges paper does not reproduce under stricter testing (ceiling 71% even after debiasing) and which in any case describes general chat preference, not holistic subjective-copy judgment.

## Open questions

- No source in the corpus tests LLM-as-judge validity on marketing/advertising copy or design-brief evaluation specifically; the essay-marking analogue is the closest available proxy but is not a direct measurement, and the disconfirming-evidence test above names exactly the study that would close this gap.
- The corpus does not contain a case study of a prompt-registry or feature-flag-adjacent system that actually separates "prompt-text RBAC" from "contract/threshold RBAC" in production — the RBAC redesign argument here is inferred from the eval-bottleneck literature plus the criterion-referenceability framework, not observed directly in a governance case study. A follow-up locus on model-registry stage-transition RBAC (MLflow) or CMDB change-approval RBAC, read specifically for who-can-edit-what boundaries, would strengthen or falsify this.
- Cost of continuously re-running a deterministic contract against the live model (proposed third pillar above) is not costed anywhere in the corpus for this locus; Test-Before-You-Deploy flags "computational overhead and operational complexity" as an open research challenge rather than a solved one.
- Whether Vietnamese-market/SEA enterprise practice (out of scope for this locus's budget) has adopted anything resembling risk-category-based contracts for LLM deployment was not investigated here.
