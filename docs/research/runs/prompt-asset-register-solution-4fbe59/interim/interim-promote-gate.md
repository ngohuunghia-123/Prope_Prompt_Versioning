# Interim report: promote-gate-efficacy-and-the-missing-approval-object

**Locus question:** Does a mandatory human approval gate help or harm here, what actually obliges it for a Vietnamese group (as opposed to an EU one), and does any existing tool implement a real approval-decision object rather than role-gated label writes?

**Flavor:** dialectical

## What the corpus already said

The width sweep already contained the DORA and code-review-theatre sources this locus was built to interrogate, plus the Langfuse/PromptLayer prompt-management docs and the EU AI Act articles ([[article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act]], [[article-18-documentation-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act]], `eu-ai-act-obligations-for-deployers-of-high-risk-ai-systems`). Critically, the corpus originally had **no Vietnamese-jurisdiction sources at all** — every legal citation available to prior analysts was EU AI Act, for a deployer that is not in the EU. That gap has now been filled with six Vietnam-specific sources; two of the most load-bearing (the Conventus Law overview of the Law on AI, and Viet An Law's Decree 142/2026/ND-CP summary) are read in full below.

## What the new sources say

**DORA capabilities page** ([[dora-capabilities-streamlining-change-approval]]): the canonical source states plainly: "no evidence was found to support the hypothesis that a more formal, external review process was associated with lower change fail rates," and that heavyweight CAB/senior-manager approval "have a negative impact on software delivery performance." Its prescribed replacement is explicit and specific: "Use peer review to meet the goal of segregation of duties, with reviews, comments, and approvals captured in the team's development platform as part of the development process," supplemented by continuous testing/CI and monitoring. DORA does not recommend removing approval — it recommends moving approval *inside* the platform and *into the hands of people with context*.

**"When DORA metrics meet governance in banking"** ([[when-dora-metrics-meet-governance-in-banking-expert-view-team-topologies-organiz]]) supplies the exact numbers the locus asked to verify. Verbatim: "organizations with formal external approval processes were found to be 2.6 times more likely to be low performers (see Accelerate State of DevOps 2019)." And, quoting *Accelerate* directly: "We found that external approvals were negatively correlated with lead time, deployment frequency, and restore time, and had no correlation with change fail rate... It is, in fact, worse than having no change approval process at all." A second data point, from the UK Financial Conduct Authority's analysis of over 1 million production changes: "CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, and in some firms the CAB had not rejected a single change during 2019. This raises questions over the effectiveness of CABs as an assurance mechanism." Note precisely what is being measured: *external, formal, decontextualized* approval bodies. Nothing in either source measures the effect of approval by an accountable owner embedded in the workflow.

**"The End of Code Review"** (arXiv:2606.13175, [[the-end-of-code-review-coding-agents-supersede-human-inspection]]) argues from a different angle — throughput, not formality. Its cost baseline: "Developers at large organisations spend between ten and fifteen percent of their working hours reading and commenting on others' code" (citing Sadowski et al., Google 2018), and it argues that as agent-driven throughput rises, "reviews of agent-generated code become rubber-stamps: the human approves because the code looks correct, because the tests pass, and because the cognitive cost of genuine scrutiny is prohibitive." Its prescription is not "no human review," it is a scope cut: "Human approval is not eliminated; it is reserved for the decisions that genuinely require it: high-risk changes, novel architecture choices, and regulated code paths where a named human must bear legal accountability. For the vast majority of commits... agent sign-off is sufficient." This is a volume/throughput argument, not a formality argument — it is orthogonal to DORA's finding, not a restatement of it.

**Rubber-Stamp Reviews (minware)** ([[rubber-stamp-reviews-minware]]) gives the failure *signature* the locus asked for, but **not a quantified size threshold** — this is worth recording as an audited absence. The source lists qualitative root causes ("PRs too large or unclear to reasonably review in one sitting," "incentives to approve quickly") and qualitative warning signs ("PRs approved within minutes of being opened," "repetitive 'LGTM' comments with no substance," "reviewers frequently approving code they have not read") but gives no number (e.g., no "X lines" or "Y changes/day" threshold). I searched the note specifically for a numeric threshold and found none; any claim of an "exact threshold" from this source would be fabricated.

**Vietnam Law on AI + Decree** ([[companies-developing-and-using-ai-in-vietnam-key-legal-obligations-under-the-new]], [[update-decree-1422026nd-cp-guiding-the-artificial-intelligence-law-in-vietnam-vi]]): Law No. 134/2025/QH15 took effect 1 March 2026; Decree 142/2026/ND-CP was issued 30 April 2026 and took effect 1 May 2026 — both dates match the run's stated context. The Law splits obligated parties into four roles (Art. 3): developers, providers, deployers, users, and applies to "all activities relating to the research, development, provision, deployment and use of AI systems in Vietnam" (Art. 1–2) — Vietnamese *and* foreign entities. For high-risk systems, Article 14 imposes six deployer obligations verbatim: "(i) operate and monitor the system in accordance with its intended purpose, scope and classified risk level; (ii) ensure safety, data security and maintain the possibility of human intervention during use; (iii) maintain compliance with applicable standards and technical regulations...; (iv) fulfil obligations relating to transparency and incident handling; (v) fulfil accountability obligations to competent state authorities...; (vi) cooperate with the provider and competent state authorities." Documentation obligations for high-risk systems span "risk management, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight mechanisms, activity log retention, system monitoring and incident management."

The Decree adds the operative mechanics: risk classification into three tiers (high/medium/low) must occur "before putting the system into operation" (Decree Art. 5, per Law Art. 9), via self-assessment or a conformity-assessment body; only medium- and high-risk systems must be notified to the Ministry of Science and Technology via a one-stop portal — low-risk systems face no notification requirement ("The law encourages... to publicize basic system information," not requires). On transition: "For any AI system put into operation before March 1, 2026... Within 60 days from the effective date of the Decree (i.e., before June 30, 2026), transitioning organizations and enterprises must submit a notice on the one-stop portal along with a transition plan or compliance scheme." **This is a 60-day notification deadline, not the 12–18 month full-compliance transition window given in the run's background context** — I could not find a statement of a 12–18 month figure in either Vietnam source I read; this should be treated as unverified until a source stating it directly is located. On scope-narrowing for internal tools, the Decree's labeling rules (Art. 18, deepfake/synthetic-media labeling) list four exemptions, one being: "Internal use: Circulating solely within an agency or organization without public release, which is particularly critical for startups building internal AI tools." This is an exemption from the *AI-generated-content labeling* obligation specifically — it is not a general carve-out of internal tools from risk classification, documentation, or logging obligations.

**EU AI Act Article 12** ([[article-12-record-keeping-eu-artificial-intelligence-act]]), read directly to compare mechanism against Vietnam: "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system," to enable traceability for risk-monitoring and post-market surveillance (Arts. 79, 72, 26(5)). This is a *logging* mandate on the AI system's own operation — not a human-sign-off-before-release mandate. The EU Act's actual human-oversight article is Art. 14 (not in my read set this round), which other investigators in this run are positioned to cover; based on the Article 12 text alone, the EU record-keeping requirement and the Vietnamese Decree's "activity log retention" requirement are structurally parallel — both mandate automated event logs on the *deployed system*, not on the *prompt-change-approval process* upstream of it.

**Langfuse Version Control** ([[version-control-langfuse]]): "protected prompt labels" are explicitly an RBAC feature gated behind Enterprise / Teams add-on tiers. Verbatim: "Protected prompt labels give project admins and owners... the ability to prevent labels from being modified or deleted, ensuring better control over prompt deployment. Once a label such as production is marked as protected: viewer and member roles cannot modify or delete the label from prompts... admin and owner roles can still modify or delete the label, effectively changing the production prompt version." Promotion = an admin/owner directly re-pointing a label at a different version ID. The docs mention comparing versions on a dataset "before promoting a label" via Experiments — a *suggested practice*, not an enforced precondition. There is no pending-review state, no required justification field, no reviewer distinct from actor, no rejection path.

**PromptLayer Release Labels** ([[release-labels-promptlayer]]): mechanically identical pattern — "hover over a prompt template version, click 'Add Release Label'" — a direct UI write action. The doc's stated purpose is progressive delivery / A/B routing via Dynamic Release Labels, not governance; no approval workflow is described anywhere in this note.

**Langfuse GitHub Discussion #4284** ([[update-prompt-label-programatically-langfuse-discussion-4284-github]]) supplies unusually concrete confirmation at the implementation-detail level: as late as Nov 2024–Jan 2025, there was no API for updating a prompt's label — a user's "temporary solution" was raw SQL against a Postgres `prompts` table (`UPDATE prompts SET labels = ...`). Maintainer `maxdeichmann` confirmed in Jan 2025 label-updates finally shipped "via our API / SDKs." This is decisive: for well over a year, Langfuse's own community had to hand-roll database writes to move a label — there was never an approval *object*, only a labels *column*.

**Amazon Bedrock Prompt Management** ([[create-a-prompt-using-prompt-management-amazon-bedrock]]): prompts move through a `DRAFT` state, then "Create version" snapshots an immutable numbered version; deployment/rollout is gated by IAM console/API permissions, structurally the same "permission-to-write" pattern as Langfuse's RBAC labels, not a review workflow. No pending-review, evidence-record, or rejection-path entity appears anywhere in the note.

## Evidence synthesis

On efficacy, the two DORA sources are unambiguous about *what* they measured: external, formal, decontextualized approval (CAB or senior manager sign-off divorced from the change). The 2.6x low-performer multiplier and the "no correlation with change fail rate" finding attach specifically to that shape of gate, and DORA's own prescribed fix is not "no approval" but "peer review... captured in the team's development platform." The FCA's ">90% approved, sometimes zero rejections in a year" statistic is the empirical signature of a gate that has become theatre — but that theatre is produced by *removal from context*, not by the existence of a sign-off step. The arXiv paper's throughput argument is a genuinely separate mechanism (rubber-stamping as a function of volume outstripping reviewer bandwidth) that happens to converge on a similar prescription: keep humans at the *few* decisions that need a named accountable person, automate the rest. Both papers, read together, converge on the same shape of surviving gate: narrow scope, embedded in the platform, held by someone with context, evidenced rather than blind.

On jurisdiction, the newly-fetched Vietnam sources change the report's framing materially. Neither the EU AI Act (Art. 12, on the mechanism read here) nor the Vietnamese Law/Decree impose a "human must approve promoting a prompt version" requirement on an internal tooling layer. Both regimes regulate the *deployed AI system* — automated event logging (EU Art. 12; VN Decree "activity log retention") and, in Vietnam's case, "the possibility of human intervention during use" (VN Law Art. 14(ii)) — a runtime override/kill-switch capacity, not a pre-release sign-off gate. Whether either regime binds *at all* turns on whether the platform's downstream consumers — the actual customer-facing AI products the group's teams build using the served prompts — clear the medium/high-risk classification threshold; if none do, notification to Vietnam's Ministry of Science and Technology isn't even triggered, and the label-based labeling exemption for "internal use" further narrows what applies. The prompt asset register itself, as a tool, most plausibly sits outside the regulated "AI system" boundary the same way a model registry, CI pipeline, or CMS sits outside it — it is infrastructure that *helps* downstream deployers produce the documentation the law asks for, not itself the obligated party.

On the missing object, three independently-read vendor sources (Langfuse docs, Langfuse's own GitHub discussion, PromptLayer docs) plus Bedrock's mechanism converge on the identical pattern: "promotion" is implemented everywhere as a role-gated (RBAC/IAM) write to a pointer field (label, release label, alias/version). None of the four surfaces I read ship a pending-review state, a reviewer-evidence record, a rejection path, or an enforced (not merely displayed) binding between an eval/cost result and the promotion action.

## Committed position

The mandatory HITL promote-gate as specified should stand, but only in the shape DORA itself recommends and only on the basis of ordinary corporate segregation-of-duties governance — not on the basis of an EU or Vietnamese legal mandate, neither of which actually compels a pre-release human sign-off for an internal prompt-management layer. DORA's 2.6x-low-performer and "no correlation with change fail rate" findings attach specifically to *external, context-poor* approval (CAB/senior-manager sign-off divorced from the change); DORA's own prescribed remedy — peer review, captured in the development platform, by someone close to the change — is structurally what a named accountable owner (the "Toàn" persona) approving from a single evidence card (diff, eval delta, cost delta, blast radius) already is, not a CAB in miniature. "The End of Code Review"'s throughput-driven rubber-stamp mechanism is real but scale-dependent, and it does not bite at the volumes a corporate prompt registry will see in its first year; that paper's own carve-out — mandatory review survives for "regulated code paths where a named human must bear legal accountability" — is a closer description of a production-prompt promotion than of a routine code commit. Separately, and this is the more consequential finding for the report's framing: the Vietnamese Law on AI (134/2025/QH15) and Decree 142/2026/ND-CP regulate *AI systems* via risk classification, documentation, and runtime human-intervention capacity — they do not regulate an internal prompt-versioning tool's release process, and the "internal use" exemption in the Decree applies narrowly to AI-generated-content labeling, not to a general carve-out. The honest legal basis for the HITL constraint is therefore prophylactic and organizational (protects against the group's future high-risk AI products failing their own Article 14/Decree documentation obligations, and satisfies ordinary internal-controls segregation of duties) rather than a specific statutory command that binds the register itself today. On the tooling side, the "missing approval object" finding is confirmed and should be treated as load-bearing for the report's differentiation argument: no vendor across Langfuse, PromptLayer, or Bedrock ships a pending-review/evidence-record/rejection-path/enforced-binding object — every one of them implements "promotion" as a role-gated write to a label or version pointer, confirmed down to the database layer in Langfuse's case. This gap must be built regardless of whether the register's substrate is git-branch-based or label-based.

- **Position:** Keep the mandatory HITL gate, but redesign it as owner-held, platform-embedded, evidence-loaded peer review (DORA's actual recommendation) rather than a CAB-style external checkpoint; ground its justification in corporate segregation-of-duties governance and prophylactic AI-Law-readiness, not in an actual present legal mandate on the register itself, since neither the EU AI Act (Art. 12 as read) nor Vietnam's Law/Decree obligates a promote-to-production human sign-off for an internal prompt-management layer.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high on the DORA reading (both DORA sources and the arXiv paper converge cleanly, 3 for, 0 against); medium on the jurisdictional non-obligation claim (2 Vietnam sources read in full, both consistent, but I have not read VN Law/Decree provisions on internal tooling scope exhaustively, nor Article 14 of the EU AI Act on human oversight, nor the Baker McKenzie/Duane Morris/OneTrust Vietnam sources listed in the corpus index but not opened this round); high on the missing-approval-object claim (4 independent vendor sources, fully convergent, one corroborated at database-schema granularity).
- **Boundary conditions:** The "DORA doesn't penalize approval per se" reading holds specifically where the approver is (a) a single accountable owner with context, not an external body, (b) supplied with pre-computed evidence rather than expected to reconstruct it, and (c) operating at volumes low enough that review doesn't become the throughput bottleneck the arXiv paper describes (plausible for a conglomerate-wide prompt registry in year one; would need revisiting if promotion volume scales to hundreds/week). The jurisdictional non-obligation reading holds for the *platform itself*; it does NOT hold for the downstream AI products built on served prompts if any of them clear Vietnam's medium/high-risk threshold or the EU's Annex III high-risk categories — those products' deployers do face documentation, logging, and human-intervention-capacity obligations, and the register's approval record is a plausible (not confirmed-required) piece of evidence for satisfying them.
- **What would change this position:** On efficacy — a study specifically measuring change-failure or lead-time outcomes for *embedded, evidence-loaded, single-owner* approval (as opposed to external CAB review) showing the same negative correlation DORA found for CABs; I found no such study and specifically looked for one. On jurisdiction — a provision in the Vietnamese Law, Decree, or an implementing circular explicitly naming "prompt management," "internal AI tooling," or "AI development platforms" as in-scope AI systems independent of the products they feed (I did not find one in the two sources read, but I did not exhaustively search the Decree's Articles 6–25 or the un-read Baker McKenzie/Duane Morris/OneTrust sources). On the missing object — discovery of a shipped feature in Vertex AI/Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, Braintrust, or a newer Langfuse/PromptLayer release that implements a genuine pending-review + evidence-record + rejection-path state machine; I did not read the Vertex/Gemini prompt-management note or Bedrock's version/alias-detail note this round, so this remains the single largest unclosed gap in this locus.
- **Evidence weight:** DORA efficacy claim — 2 primary DORA-lineage sources (dora.dev, Team Topologies quoting Accelerate + FCA data) plus 1 corroborating throughput-argument paper (arXiv 2606.13175), 0 contradicting; rubber-stamp threshold claim — explicitly unquantified in the only source read (audited absence, not a number to report). Jurisdiction claim — 2 Vietnam primary/secondary legal sources read in full and internally consistent, 1 EU AI Act article read directly for comparison; 4 additional Vietnam sources in the corpus index not read this round (baker mckenzie, duane morris, vietnam-briefing/onetrust, vietanlaw's other AI pieces) represent unclosed verification surface, particularly for the disputed 12–18 month transition-period figure, which I could not corroborate. Missing-object claim — 3 vendor sources read in full (Langfuse docs + GitHub discussion, PromptLayer docs) plus 1 corroborating mechanism source (Bedrock), 0 sources found describing a real approval-decision object; 2 sources in the suggested set (Vertex/Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform prompt management, Bedrock's version-detail page) not read this round.

## Open questions

- Does any source state Vietnam Law 134/2025/QH15 was passed on 10 Dec 2025 specifically (National Assembly passage date)? Neither Vietnam source I read states a passage date, only the 1 March 2026 effective date. Worth a targeted check against the Baker McKenzie or Duane Morris notes.
- Is the "transition period of 12–18 months" in the run's background context correct? What I found in the Decree note is a 60-day notification deadline (by 30 June 2026) for systems already operating before 1 March 2026 — not a 12–18 month compliance window. This should be reconciled against the un-read Baker McKenzie/Duane Morris/vietnam-briefing sources before the final report states a transition-period figure.
- Does EU AI Act Article 14 (Human Oversight, not read this round) impose anything closer to a pre-release human sign-off than Article 12's logging mandate does? This matters for the EU-side half of the jurisdictional comparison and should be checked by whichever investigator owns the EU AI Act deployer-obligations locus.
- Does Vertex AI / Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform's prompt management (not read this round) implement anything closer to a real approval object than Langfuse/PromptLayer/Bedrock? Given how consistent the "role-gated write, no workflow object" pattern is across 4 independent surfaces, I'd assign low probability that Vertex breaks the pattern, but it is unverified.
- Does any Vietnamese circular or Ministry guidance (below decree level) specifically address internal developer/AI-tooling platforms, as opposed to customer-facing AI products? Not found in the two sources read; the "internal use" exemption found applies only to synthetic-media labeling.
