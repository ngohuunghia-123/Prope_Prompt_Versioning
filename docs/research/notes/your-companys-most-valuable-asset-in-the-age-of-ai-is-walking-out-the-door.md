Summary
Frontier models are commoditizing, and the model itself will differentiate no one. What will differentiate is the institutional knowledge embedded in it, and that knowledge is produced every time our teams correct an AI output, because those corrections are the reward signal reinforcement learning runs on. The catch is that this data flows through whoever hosts the workflow, and the economics of their model business reward pulling it into weights that get sold to everyone, including your competitors. Three practical steps to ensure durable advantage, preventing companies from renting back their own knowledge, by the token.

Turning institutional knowledge into model capability
Joshua Sortina
Generic AI models will become cheap, capable, and available to everyone, which means they will differentiate no one. The question I keep working through with boards is where advantage moves next. The answer is hiding in plain sight - in the operational record every company produces daily: how it orchestrates its processes, what it changes in an AI recommendation, what it accepts, and what it rejects. That record is training data. It may be the most valuable IP your company creates this decade.
And most companies are giving it away.
The human-in-the-loop is not oversight. It is the training mechanism.
To see why this data matters, look at how frontier models actually improve. Pretraining on public internet data is table stakes, and the real capability gains now come from reinforcement learning (RL) applied after pretraining. RL requires a reward signal, and the best reward signal anyone has found is a human expert correcting the model inside a real workflow. Every time one of your employees edits an AI-drafted proposal, overrides a recommendation, or approves an exception, they are producing a labeled training example that encodes judgment no public dataset contains.
We have been describing human-in-the-loop as a safety checkpoint. It is better understood as the flywheel that turns institutional knowledge into model capability. The economics back this up: base models trained on the same public web are converging in capability,
the share of frontier training compute going to RL is rising across labs
, and the scaling lever is turning out to be reward and environment quality rather than algorithms.
Expert corrections are precisely the reward signal in shortest supply.
Satya Nadella made this argument
in his recent memo on human capital and token capital, and it deserves a careful read by anyone in a governance seat: "This loop becomes the new IP of the firm. I think of it as a hill climbing machine. And unlike most assets, it compounds."

When models commoditize, distilled models become the moat.
Jensen Huang arrives at the same place from the infrastructure side
. Every company is built on domain-specific intellectual property, he argues, and "outsourcing this intelligence makes no sense." He calls a company's proprietary agents its "crown jewel," and his description of the shift is the most quotable line in enterprise computing right now: "Your data center used to be a data center for files. It's now a factory for tokens."
The market has already priced this in.
Meta paid $14.3 billion for Scale AI
, a company whose core business is curated human feedback and reinforcement learning environments. When the scarce asset was compute, the premiums went to chips. The premiums are now moving to exactly the kind of data your operations generate for free.
Your provider's incentive runs against yours, and that is a business model rather than bad faith.
Here is the uncomfortable part for anyone who has signed a services or software agreement recently. A process outsourcer captures your loop data by running your processes. An application provider captures it by hosting the workflow where every accept and reject decision happens. Same exposure, two doors, and many agreements in force today never contemplated either one.
What makes this harder than an ordinary vendor negotiation is that the misalignment is structural. The economics of the model business reward moving intelligence out of customer workflows and into model weights, because capability encoded in the weights can be sold to everyone. Nobody has to act in bad faith for that to happen; it is simply how the business works.
But it means the same corrections that would make your model distinctly better also make a shared model better for whoever else uses it, including the competitor down the road who never did the work. It is also why a number of model and application providers have begun offering capabilities that overlap with the businesses of the enterprises they serve. Waiting for standard terms to settle is not a strategy here, because the terms are being written right now by whoever moves first, and the time to write yours is before the loops are running.
The outsourcing wave moved process knowledge far from the core. AI forces a rethink.
There is a deeper structural issue beyond the contracts. Over two decades we outsourced enormous swaths of our business processes, and in doing so we moved the people who understand the nuances of those processes far from the center of the firm. That was rational when the test was cost.
In the age of AI the test has changed, because the corrections and judgments of exactly those people are now the raw material of proprietary model capability. Nadella draws the parallel himself, warning that companies renting their AI capability risk being hollowed out the way manufacturing was in the last wave. The sourcing question is no longer what is cheap to move out. It is what learning must stay close to the core, and that question deserves a fresh look at the entire source-in, source-out map.
The question is not what kind of company you are. It is how many learning loops you can see.
Play this forward and the interesting distinction turns out not to be industry or business model, but vantage point. An operating company sees one set of loops, its own, which is deep but singular. An enterprise software provider or a process operator sees the loops of its entire install base, because every customer's accept and reject decisions run through its workflows.
Whoever sits across many loops, and holds the rights to learn across them, ends up with a model no single participant could build alone. That is not a prediction about who wins. It is a description of where the aggregation points already are, and many of them are being occupied under agreements written before anyone thought of operational corrections as training data. None of this is a reason to stop working with partners. It is a reason to count carefully: how many loops can you see, and how many can others see through you.
The work ahead starts with a thoughtful inventory, runs through a loop, and ends in a more durable company.
So what does a board and executive team actually do with this?
The first move is an inventory of your assets for the AI age. Map where institutional knowledge actually lives: which processes generate expert corrections at scale, which decisions encode judgment that exists nowhere else, and which of these would make a distilled model dangerous to compete against. Then overlay two dimensions on that map. Distance asks how far each pocket of knowledge now sits from the core after two decades of sourcing decisions, and whether it needs to come closer. Contracts ask who captures the learning wherever that knowledge meets an AI workflow, and whether the training rights, ownership of improvements, and data boundaries are written down anywhere.
The second move is a learning loop plan, and the good news is that starting it is less exotic than it sounds. The components you need are clear: private evaluations that measure AI against your outcomes rather than public benchmarks, reinforcement learning environments built from traces of real workflows, and internal knowledge bases your systems can query. You do not need all of it on day one. Pick one high-volume workflow where your teams already correct AI output, capture the accepts, rejects, and edits systematically instead of letting them evaporate, and measure whether the system improves against results you care about. The design test Nadella proposes is worth adopting as policy: you should be able to swap the generalist model underneath without losing the company veteran your loop has trained.
The third move is the point of the first two. A corporation whose judgment lives only in its people loses a little of itself with every departure, and a corporation whose judgment lives in a vendor's platform never owned itself to begin with. A corporation that encodes its judgment into systems it owns, has for the first time, a form of institutional knowledge that survives turnover, survives reorganizations, and survives model generations. That is what a sustainable company looks like in the AI age, and it is being decided now, workflow by workflow, contract by contract. The companies that take inventory and build their learning loops early will own them.
The rest will rent back their own knowledge, by the token.
