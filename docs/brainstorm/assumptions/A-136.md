# A-136 — A restore loses up to 24 hours of prompt text, and the history proves the loss without recovering it

**Statement**: ADR-0036 sets the whole instance at RPO 24 h — a nightly `pg_dump -Fc` copied off the
host. prompt-registry inherits it with no exclusions, and it is the module where that number costs the
most. A restore returns `registry.revision` to its state at the last dump, so **every revision created
since then is gone**, along with the `search_text` those revisions produced.

What makes this different from the other two modules that examined their backup row this pass:

- `ledger.call_body` is excluded from the dump entirely (ADR-0039) and has **no read path at all**
  (ADR-0018), so losing it costs nothing anybody could have used.
- `access.session` is excluded deliberately (ADR-0044), and a lost session is re-obtained by logging
  in.
- **A prompt body has no second source.** It is not derivable, not re-issuable, and not held anywhere
  else in the system. It is the asset the product exists to hold.

The sharp edge is what the audit trail does. property-history's `revision_created` event records that
a revision existed — its number, its author, its timestamp, its change summary — but **never its body**,
because A-046 forbids a prompt body in a history event. Those events are in the same dump, so they roll
back too. Any event written after the dump is lost with the revision it describes; any event written
before it survives and stays true.

The result is a register that can tell you precisely which revisions were lost, by number and author
and stated reason, and cannot give you a single character of their text back.

**Rationale**: The alternative is a second backup mechanism for one table — continuous archiving, or a
more frequent dump of `registry` alone — and ADR-0044 already rejected the narrower version of that
move for `access`, on the ground that a second schedule narrows the window without closing it while
doubling the surface that can fail silently. The honest position at pilot scale is that 24 h is
accepted, not mitigated, and that the people using Prope are told so rather than left to discover it.

Two things make it survivable in the pilot and neither is a mitigation: A-020 bars real corporate
prompts until OQ-006's baseline is signed off, so the text at risk today is test material; and prompt
authors typically still hold what they wrote somewhere outside Prope during a pilot, which stops being
true the moment Prope becomes the system of record — which is the entire goal.

**Impact if wrong**: If a restore never happens, this costs a paragraph. If one happens after Prope is
the system of record, the loss is unbounded in importance and bounded only in time, and the recovery is
to ask each author to rewrite what they wrote in the preceding day from memory. The recorded procedural
step is therefore: **after any restore, export the surviving `revision_created` events for the RPO
window and give each author the list of their own lost revisions, with change summaries, before anyone
resumes work** — the history cannot recover the text, but it can name exactly who lost what, and that
is the difference between a recoverable day and a silent one.

The trigger that changes the answer is OQ-006's sign-off. At that point the correct move is
PostgreSQL WAL archiving / point-in-time recovery for the whole instance, which lowers the RPO for
every module at once rather than special-casing this one. ADR-0036 rejected it *"for the pilot, not on
merit"*, calling it *"the technically better answer — RPO of minutes rather than a day"* and **cheap to
add later**: `archive_mode`, an `archive_command`, and a fresh base backup, with no schema change, no
data movement, and no application change. This assumption is the record of who pays for that deferral.
