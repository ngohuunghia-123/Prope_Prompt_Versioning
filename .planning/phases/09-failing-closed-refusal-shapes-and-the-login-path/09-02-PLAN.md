<objective>
Bound how much argon2id this box is willing to run, without giving an attacker a way to tell that they
were bounded.

ADR-0043 is unusually explicit about what the limiter is for, and it is not what the name suggests:
*"The rate limiter's real job is therefore not to protect the password. **It is to bound how much
argon2id the box is willing to run**, and that only works if it rejects before the hash rather than
after it."* On ADR-0033's 4 vCPU / 8 GB host, one verification is 46 MiB and — at `parallelism=1`, which
ADR-0043 chose specifically so it is not four — one core for 55-70 ms, inside the process A-130 says
also serves the gateway. An unthrottled login is therefore a resource amplifier pointed at the whole
product, and phase 8 shipped exactly that for one phase (D-128, T-08-25) with the exposure declared.

**This plan also closes the third of PITFALLS gap 1's three owed access-control criteria.** Its text:
*"the login limiter performs no argon2id verification at threshold even with the correct password,
asserted by timing or a hasher spy."* Phase 7 routed it here in writing (`07/deferred-items.md` §5a
row (c)) because a test written in Phase 7 would have asserted against something that did not exist and
would then have had to be deleted here. **It is asserted both ways** — a spy and a clock — because the
spy proves the call did not happen and the clock proves nothing equivalent happened by another route.

**D-135 — The limiter is one bounded structure keyed on `(bucket, key)`, with the clock injected.**

Chosen: a module-level `LoginLimiter` holding `dict[tuple[str, str], deque[float]]`, timestamps taken
from `time.monotonic()` through an injected `clock: Callable[[], float]` parameter defaulting to
`time.monotonic`. `check_and_record(bucket, key) -> bool` prunes entries older than `LOGIN_WINDOW`,
answers whether the count is at the limit, and records the attempt if it is not. `MAX_TRACKED_KEYS`
caps the dict; when it is exceeded, the **oldest-touched** keys are evicted. A module-level singleton
plus `reset_limiter()` as a test seam, in the register `src/prope/call_ledger/enqueue.py` already
established for module-level state built once with a reset hook.

Rejected: **`time.time()`.** A wall clock moves backwards on an NTP correction and a window computed
across a backwards step either expires everything or nothing. `monotonic` is what a duration is
measured with.

Rejected: **a fixed-window counter** (a count plus a period start). It admits up to twice the limit
across a boundary — 5 at 14:59 and 5 at 15:01 — and A-144's number is *"in a rolling 15-minute
window"*, which is the sliding form and not the fixed one.

Rejected: **an unbounded dict.** An attacker cycling email addresses would grow it without bound in the
process serving the gateway, which is the same failure the 512-record handoff bound exists to prevent
one module over. `MAX_TRACKED_KEYS` is set to **20,000** with the arithmetic at the constant: each entry
is a short deque of floats, the address bucket admits 20 before rejecting so a deque never exceeds 20
entries, and 20,000 × 20 floats is a few megabytes — small against the 46 MiB a single verification
allocates, which is the resource actually being defended.

Reversibility: **cheap.**

**D-136 — The source address is the transport peer, and a forwarded header is trusted only when a
setting says how many hops to trust.**

This is a genuine two-way decision with a security answer and a deployment answer that disagree, so it
is written as a checkpoint below and adopted rather than assumed.

A-129 makes Caddy the single ingress, so **every request the `app` process sees has Caddy's address as
its transport peer** and a per-source limit keyed on that peer is a limit on one address for the whole
internet — worthless. The real client address is in `X-Forwarded-For`, which Caddy sets. But
`X-Forwarded-For` is a request header, and on Prope's **only unauthenticated route** a client that can
set it can pick a fresh source address per attempt and the 20-per-address limit becomes no limit at all.

Chosen: `Settings` gains `trusted_proxy_hops: int = 0` (env `PROPE_TRUSTED_PROXY_HOPS`). At `0` the
source key is the ASGI scope's transport peer and `X-Forwarded-For` is ignored entirely. At `n > 0` the
key is the **n-th entry from the right** of `X-Forwarded-For` — counting from the right because the
rightmost entries are the ones each trusted proxy appended, and everything to the left of them is
client-supplied and forgeable. A missing or short header at `n > 0` falls back to the transport peer,
which is a **narrower** key, never a wider one.

Rejected: **always trusting the leftmost `X-Forwarded-For` entry.** It is the standard recipe and it is
the standard vulnerability: the leftmost entry is whatever the client sent.

Rejected: **always using the transport peer.** Correct in this repository's test rig and wrong the
moment Caddy is in front of it, in the direction that silently disables the control. The default is `0`
because the code must be safe before the deployment exists (PITFALLS §4 — the deployment stack owns no
milestone and nobody may be relied on to set the value), and the failure at `0` behind a proxy is
**over-limiting**, which is loud, rather than under-limiting, which is silent.

Consequence, stated: at `0` behind Caddy, twenty failed logins from anywhere block every source. That is
a denial of service on the login screen and it is the direction chosen deliberately. `deferred-items.md`
carries it and the deployment work must set the value.

Reversibility: **cheap** — a setting, and the parsing lives in one function.

---

**D-137 — The limiter records failures only, a rejection does not extend its own window, and success
clears the email bucket alone.**

Chosen: `check_and_record` is called once at the top of the handler in **read-only** mode
(`check_only=True`) and, when the attempt turns out to have failed, `record_failure` is called on both
buckets. A rejected attempt therefore does **not** append a timestamp, so an attacker hammering the
endpoint cannot hold their own window open indefinitely — the window rolls off 15 minutes after the
fifth *failure*, not 15 minutes after the last *attempt*.

On success: `clear(bucket="email", key=email)` — the lock file requires it in those words, *"a
successful sign-in clears the email bucket"* — and the source-address bucket is left alone, because a
source that has produced nineteen failures and one success is still a source that has produced nineteen
failures, and that is what the address bucket is counting.

Rejected: **recording every attempt.** It makes a successful login count against the limit, which locks
a person out of a product for guessing right.

Rejected: **clearing both buckets on success.** One valid credential would reset an attacker's address
budget, which is the shared-machine case the address limit exists for.

Reversibility: **cheap.**

**`src/prope/access_control/rate_limit.py` — new.**

Five module constants, each with a `#:` block in `HANDOFF_MAXSIZE`'s voice — the number, where it comes
from, and what changing it would cost:

```python
LOGIN_EMAIL_LIMIT = 5
LOGIN_ADDRESS_LIMIT = 20
LOGIN_WINDOW_SECONDS = 900.0
MAX_TRACKED_KEYS = 20_000
BUCKET_EMAIL, BUCKET_SOURCE_ADDRESS = "email", "source_address"
```

`LOGIN_EMAIL_LIMIT` and `LOGIN_ADDRESS_LIMIT` cite **A-144** and record that these numbers were set on
2026-08-09 at interfaces step 5 because *"this line and the security requirement below had both
committed to a limiter and named no number anywhere in the repository"* — a blank an implementer fills,
now filled by the user rather than by an agent, which is why they are not tunable by feel.
`LOGIN_WINDOW_SECONDS` carries the rolling-versus-fixed argument from D-135. `MAX_TRACKED_KEYS` carries
D-135's arithmetic. The two bucket names are the **label values** on
`prope_access_control_login_rate_limited_total`, so the constants and the label set are one thing.

```python
@dataclass(slots=True)
class LoginLimiter:
    clock: Callable[[], float] = time.monotonic
    def is_at_limit(self, bucket: str, key: str) -> bool
    def record_failure(self, bucket: str, key: str) -> None
    def clear(self, bucket: str, key: str) -> None
```

`is_at_limit` prunes entries older than `LOGIN_WINDOW_SECONDS` from that key's deque, then compares the
remaining count against the bucket's limit. It **records nothing** — D-137: a rejected attempt must not
extend its own window. `record_failure` prunes, appends, and enforces `MAX_TRACKED_KEYS` by evicting
the least-recently-touched keys. `clear` drops the key entirely.

Module-level `_LIMITER = LoginLimiter()`, a `login_limiter()` accessor, and `reset_limiter()` — the
test seam, in `enqueue.reset_handoff`'s register. Its docstring says what it is for and that nothing in
the shipped application calls it.

The module docstring carries, in this order:

1. **What this is for**, in ADR-0043's own terms: not protecting the password — bounding how much
   argon2id the box will run. 46 MiB and one core per verification on a 4 vCPU host, in the process
   A-130 says also serves the gateway.
2. **Why it is in the process and not in PostgreSQL or Redis or Caddy**, with all three rejections
   named — the counter table's unauthenticated write path and pruning job, Redis being ruled out by
   A-080/A-114/ADR-0033, and Caddy's `rate_limit` needing an `xcaddy` build that ADR-0031's
   pull-never-build pipeline would have to start producing, *and* being unable to express per-email at
   all because it does not see the body.
3. **This is not a cache and does not touch ADR-0022.** It holds no authentication result, permission
   decision, key scope, role binding or session — only counts of attempts — so **nothing here can
   return a stale allow, because it can only produce a denial.** That sentence is load-bearing: plan
   09-03 ships a source-level assertion that nothing in this module caches an access-control answer, and
   this file is the one that looks like it does.
4. **The per-process consequence**, with A-130's split as the trigger and the compose file as the
   detection — *"the second `app` service appearing in the compose file IS the detection"* — and
   ADR-0043's named fix, which is the option ADR-0043 itself rejected.
5. **A deploy resets the windows**, noted rather than mitigated, because deploys are ADR-0031 pulls and
   not attacker-triggered.

**`src/prope/access_control/rate_limit.py` — the address parser.**

`source_address(scope: Mapping[str, Any], *, trusted_hops: int) -> str`. At `trusted_hops == 0`, return
the ASGI scope's `client` host or a fixed `"unknown"` sentinel when there is none (an ASGI server that
supplies no client is a real case and `None` must not become a dict key that every such request shares
by accident — a named sentinel makes that sharing visible). At `n > 0`, split `X-Forwarded-For` on
commas, strip, and take the entry `n` positions from the right; on a header that is missing or has fewer
than `n` entries, **fall back to the transport peer**. Write at the function why the count is from the
right, and that everything to the left of the trusted hops is client-supplied.

Four things at this call site, each of which is the reason it exists:

- **The email key is normalised before it is used** — stripped and case-folded — because `unique(email)`
  is a database constraint on the stored value and an attacker who can vary the case can otherwise get a
  fresh bucket per attempt. Say so at the normalisation, and use the **same** normalisation the sign-in
  service uses; if they differ, that is a finding and the SUMMARY names it.
- **The refusal is the same object the wrong-password path raises**, constructed by the same helper,
  not a second construction that happens to look the same today. Extract the existing failure into one
  local `_authentication_failed()` and call it from both places. A second construction is how two
  responses drift into being distinguishable.
- **Which bucket is reported.** `login_rate_limited_total` is labelled `bucket` and exactly one of the
  two values is used per rejection; when both are at limit, report `email` and say why in a comment —
  the email bucket is the more specific of the two and reporting both would double-count a single
  rejection.
- **`login_total` is NOT incremented**, and the comment says why in ADR-0043's words: rejections happen
  before the hash so they are invisible in `login_total{result=bad_password}`, which is the series
  `AccessControlLoginBruteForce` fires on, and *"without the counter, a working limiter makes the alert
  quieter, and the more effective it is the more it looks like nothing is happening."*
